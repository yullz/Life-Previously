# Thumbnail playbook

Researched 2026-10-07 (YouTube's official guidance, published click-through studies, professional designers' methods, illustrated-explainer practice). Never model on another channel's thumbnails (hard rule 1); these are principles and data.

## Rules

1. Write the promise down first and judge every concept against it: 'a normal day in 1390 London was stranger and more regulated than you think.' The image has to show one everyday daytime activity (work, food, toilet, a rule, free time) that stands for the whole day. Never build it on the last chapter alone. [YouTube Test & Compare picks winners by watch-time share, not CTR; Creator Liaison: 'thumbnails that make a promise and videos that deliver on it'; the owner rejected AFTER DARK because it sold only the curfew]
2. Before approving a concept, write down the timestamp where its moment appears. Prefer moments the cold open sets up and the first half pays off. In ep 001 the cold open asks 'When do you start work?' (0:47) and 'What do you eat...' (0:49), and says London 'has rules for almost everything' (0:40). [YouTube CTR FAQ: high CTR with low average view duration is clickbait and gets recommended less; muted playback in feeds shows the opening seconds instead of the thumbnail]
3. Every element and every word must trace to a checked row in claims.md and to a scene in the cut: the object, the punishment, the setting, the time of day. Never state a myth as fact (for example 'water killed them'). [YouTube spam policy bans thumbnails that don't deliver what they promise; channel hard rule 2]
4. One idea, three elements: the Visitor close-up, one story object or activity, and one headline, plus the small LONDON 1390 chip. No panels, day arcs, collages or flying objects. At most three figures in focus; blur everything else into texture. [YouTube tips: 'try not to make the design too complex'; JAMS 2026, 22,958 thumbnails: inverted U, too busy and too empty both lose; Netflix: win rate 'dramatically dropped' above 3 people]
5. The Visitor stands in for the viewer. Use a chest-up close-up on a third, with head and shoulders filling about 1/3 to 1/2 of the frame height, touching or reacting to the object. When a concept needs a close-up, draw a new close-up pose. Don't crop a full-body PNG: the 1792x2400 full-body files need upscaling for a chest-up crop, and the softness shows on a TV. (Pipeline reality: `lp.py thumb` builds a 1920x1080 master and a 1280x720 JPG; episode 002 uploaded the 1920x1080 masters.) [YouTube casual-viewer tip: relatable emotions; vidIQ top 50: face about a third of the frame; Eden: a face over 50% of the frame adds nothing; YouTube TV guidance: thumbnails are 'supersized' like movie posters]
6. The emotion must come from the object: disgust at a smell, horror at a contract, a mouth burnt on a hot pie. No scream without a cause, and no smile unless it's ironic. [Eden: shocked +0.8 pts, neutral/serious +3.7, smiling -2.2; vidIQ: only about 1 in 20 breakouts use exaggerated shock; Netflix: complex emotions beat stoic or bland faces]
7. Point his gaze at the hook. When the hook is an object, turn his head and eyes toward it (flip the pose if needed). Use eye contact only when his reaction is the whole hook. [Sajjacholapunt & Ball 2014: a face looking toward the content raised dwell time and recall; Eden: eye contact +3.2 pts]
8. Headline: one to four words (bible rule, owner-approved 2026-10-07). Make it a statement, not a question, because the title already asks one. Never reuse title words (MEDIEVAL, LONDON, ALL DAY, DAY, DO). Give it a concrete number, rule, consequence or YOUR. Don't drop meaning just to save a word: 4-6 words showed no penalty. [Eden: repeating the title verbatim 6.3% vs 6.8% (no gain), 4-6 words +4.1; Kuiken 2017: question headlines did worse, pronouns did better; Molina et al. CHI 2021: questions didn't raise curiosity; Le Quere & Matias 2025: when the competing headline is vague, add concreteness]
9. Lean negative and comic, never fearful or graphic. Discomfort, disgust, punishment and comic misfortune are all good. No bodies, no drowning, no close-up flames on a person, no blood. [Robertson et al. 2023: each negative word +2.3% CTR, fear -0.7%; Oosterwijk 2017: people choose negative images; YouTube removes violent or graphic thumbnails; System1: schadenfreude works for characters]
10. Get the pop from value contrast, not new colours. Keep the earthy palette and make the orange hoodie (and the orange accent line of the headline) the only saturated elements. Put a lighter subject against a darker, slightly blurred background, and include one large bright, warm area so the image doesn't sink into a dark-mode feed. [Eden: brightness, contrast, saturation and warmth all differ by 0.6 or less on a 0-100 scale; YouTube ambient mode and dark theme; channel rule 7: no style change mid-season]
11. Keep the face, headline and key object out of the bottom-right corner (roughly the last 15% of width and 10% of height), away from the rounded corners and off the bottom edge. Put the headline top-left or top-right, on the side opposite the Visitor. [YouTube redesign: rounded corners and progress bar; duration badge sits bottom-right (size from tool blogs, weak)]
12. Check every candidate at small and large size. Look at 160-320 px wide on a white page and a near-black page (thumbnail_small.png), on a phone mock-up with the real title under it, and as the full-size master (1920x1080 from `lp.py thumb`) on a TV, looking for AI tells. If the subject, emotion and headline don't read in about a second, simplify. [YouTube counts an impression after 1 second with 50% visible; Netflix: about 1.8 seconds per title; YouTube TV tips; Beaupre: bigger home-feed thumbnails drove long-form engagement]
13. Keep the series device fixed: the same Visitor and hoodie at a similar size and side, white headline (#FFFFFF) with its last line in the Visitor's orange (`--accent`) and a dark-brown stroke, and the orange place-and-year chip every episode. [System1/IPA: campaigns with a recurring 'fluent device' character were 73% more likely to report large profit gains; YouTube via Creator Insider: keep a consistent series look while showing what each episode is about]
14. Sketch before spending. Rough out 10-15 concepts as code composites from existing poses and placeholder backgrounds. Score them against rule 1, the three-element limit and the squint test. Only then render 2-3 structurally different backgrounds, plus any new close-up poses, stating the count and cost first. Export with `lp.py thumb` (1920x1080 master, 1280x720 JPG), copy each result into the episode's `thumbs/` folder (the command overwrites `thumbnail.jpg`), and upload the master from desktop. [Craft practice: concept-first, many sketches (Spotter, Hawkins); YouTube Add thumbnails spec: 3840x2160, 50 MB from desktop; channel hard rule 5]
15. Test layouts, not tweaks, and don't panic-swap. Put the favourite in slot 1 and make the other slots structurally different concepts (for example work / punishment / food). Expect 'Inconclusive' on a zero-subscriber channel. Judge CTR together with average view duration, remembering that half of all channels sit at 2-10%. [YouTube A/B help: winner by watch-time share, similar variants run longer, Inconclusive keeps the first upload; Rene Ritchie: test big differences first; YouTube CTR FAQ]

## Evidence (by research angle)

### YouTube official
- **YouTube chooses thumbnail winners by watch time share, not click-through rate, so the thumbnail's promise has to match what the whole video delivers.** (strong) Test & Compare (now A/B testing of titles and thumbnails, up to 3 options) shows each option to an equal share of viewers. It declares a 'Winner' only when one option 'clearly outperformed the others based on watch time share'. Its own wording is that it optimizes 'for overall watch time over other metrics, like click-through-rate'. YouTube's Creator Liaison gave the reason: 'If you over-index on CTR, it could become click-bait, which could tank retention.' He described the goal as 'thumbnails that make a promise and videos that deliver on it.' What this means for episode 001: a thumbnail that only sells the curfew ending (the 'AFTER DARK?' concept) can pull in viewers who want night-time dread. They then sit through dawn, breakfast and work before the payoff, and that lowers watch time share. The best concept sells the title's 'all day': a day packed with surprising, grim or funny realities. Its hook should be one the video pays off early and keeps paying off, such as the Visitor reacting to something true from the first few minutes, rather than only the last chapter.  
  Sources: YouTube Help, Test & compare thumbnails - https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13861714; YouTube Help, A/B test titles & thumbnails - https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16391400?hl=en; YouTube Blog, Rene's Top Five (June 14, 2024), Creator Liaison on thumbnail testing - https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/renes-top-five-june-14-2024/
- **YouTube defines clickbait as high CTR with low average view duration, recommends it less, and treats maliciously misleading thumbnails as a spam-policy violation.** (strong) The CTR help page says: 'Clickbait videos tend to have low average view duration and therefore are less likely to get recommended by YouTube.' It also says: 'You can tell if your thumbnail is clickbait if it's getting high CTR but low average view duration.' The spam policy bans 'maliciously misleading titles, thumbnails, descriptions, or imagery to trick users into clicking on a video that does not deliver what was promised'. Its example is a title that promises a full match when the video is only a clip. Penalties range from a warning or strike up to termination. Rule for this channel: every element in the thumbnail must appear in the video and match its fact-checked claims. That includes the object, the punishment, the setting and the time of day. The headline must not overstate a claim either: the 'water killed them' myth is only half true, so the thumbnail must not state it as fact. Ideally the depicted moment comes early enough that the click is rewarded quickly. Strong true images are available, for example the short-weight baker dragged on a hurdle with the loaf round his neck, rotten meat burned under a seller's nose in the pillory, or Richard the Raker's cesspit. Any of these is allowed as long as the video shows it.  
  Sources: YouTube Help, Impressions and click-through rate FAQs - https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154; YouTube Help, Spam, deceptive practices & scams policies - https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973
- **Recommendations rest on appeal, engagement and satisfaction, and YouTube advises framing niche topics as everyday, relatable value.** (strong) YouTube says the system tries to judge whether viewers 'would be interested in the video and feel satisfied after watching it'. It uses three groups of signals: Appeal (did people choose to watch, or ignore it and click 'not interested'), Engagement (did they stick around) and Satisfaction (did they enjoy it). YouTube says the title and thumbnail 'are vital for communicating value, sparking intrigue, and setting clear expectations.' Its worked example rewrites a niche analysis of the coffee market as 'Why Is Your Coffee So Expensive?', aimed at the viewer's own life. For this channel, the thumbnail should say 'ordinary daily life, and it would be you': a relatable everyday situation (bed, food, work, toilet, getting home) made strange by the period. It should not look like a lesson about kings, battles or dates. The Visitor, a modern person standing in for the viewer, is the device that delivers this 'it's you' framing.  
  Sources: YouTube Help, Understand your content performance for YouTube's recommendation system - https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16559650?hl=en
- **When targeting casual (non-subscriber) viewers, lead with universally relatable actions and emotions, and keep the design simple and readable.** (moderate) YouTube's own tips: content for casual viewers 'might focus on actions and emotions that are more universally relatable, like a shocked face'. Use the rule of thirds, overlay readable text with an easy-to-read font, and 'try not to make the design too complex'. YouTube also notes that '90% of the best-performing videos have custom thumbnails.' A brand-new channel gets almost all its traffic from browse and suggested, so this is the case to design for. Make one large Visitor close-up reaction the emotional anchor, sitting on a third and filling roughly a third to half of the frame height (closeup-horrified-contract, closeup-terrified, closeup-worried; draw a new close-up pose if the concept needs a different emotion). Put one clear subject or object on the other third that explains the reaction, and use a short readable headline. Avoid busy multi-panel layouts. The rejected three-panel 'WAKE. WORK. CURFEW.' and the day-arc street both break the 'not too complex' guidance at feed size.  
  Sources: YouTube Help, Thumbnail & title tips - https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12340300?hl=en
- **Design for big screens and small ones together: YouTube asks for clean, poster-like thumbnails that work on every device, and has been making home-feed thumbnails larger.** (strong) YouTube: 'Thumbnails show up differently across devices, so make sure your thumbnail image is as large as possible.' Its TV guidance: 'Think of your thumbnails as movie posters! On TV screens, they're supersized, front and center... keep your designs clean, balanced and easy to read', and 'less is often more on the big screen'. It suggests previewing on a real TV. YouTube's growth director Todd Beaupré said the desktop home page moved to larger thumbnails with fewer per row because 'Sometimes choice is overwhelming'. He said the change 'drove more attention and engagement around long-form.' Two checks before upload. (a) Shrink the thumbnail to about 160-320 px wide and confirm the subject, the emotion and the headline still read. (b) View the 3840x2160 file full-screen on a TV and confirm nothing gives it away as AI, since the TV shows the 4K background very large and any model artifact becomes visible.  
  Sources: YouTube Help, Thumbnail & title tips - https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12340300?hl=en; YouTube Blog, How to optimize your YouTube channel for the TV screen - https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/tv-tips/; Search Engine Journal, YouTube bigger thumbnails drove long-form engagement (Beaupré on Creator Insider) - https://searchenginejournal.com/youtube-bigger-thumbnails-long-form-engagement/588102
- **An impression counts after only about a second of visibility, so the thumbnail must be understood almost at a glance.** (moderate) YouTube counts an impression when a thumbnail is shown for more than 1 second with at least 50% of it on screen, on desktop, TV, consoles, Android and iOS, in search, on the home page, in feeds and in Up Next. That counting rule comes straight from YouTube. The design conclusion is an inference: in roughly one second a scrolling viewer takes in one focal point, one emotion and a few words at most. So keep the headline to about 1-3 words, give it the highest contrast in the image, and let the image carry the story without the viewer reading the title.  
  Sources: YouTube Help, Check your YouTube impressions and watch time - https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314486?hl=en
- **Current official specs are 16:9 at up to 3840x2160 with a minimum width of 640 px. Test & Compare needs every option at 720p or above.** (strong) YouTube now recommends a 3840x2160 resolution for video thumbnails (minimum width 640 px) at 16:9, as JPG or PNG. The size limit is 50MB when uploading from desktop and 2MB when uploading from mobile. YouTube raised the limit from 2MB to 50MB in 2025 so it could serve 4K thumbnails on TVs. In Test & Compare, if any one option is below 1280x720, every option is downscaled to 854x480. For this pipeline: render the code-overlaid headline at 3840x2160, export every A/B variant at the same 4K resolution as a JPG under 50MB, and upload from desktop.

- **YouTube's interface covers and trims parts of every thumbnail, so keep the face and headline in the safe area.** (moderate) Every feed thumbnail has a duration badge in the bottom-right corner. Returning viewers also see a red progress bar along the bottom edge. YouTube's redesign rounded thumbnail corners, with corner size scaling to the element. In dark theme, ambient mode samples, blurs and stretches the video's colours into a glow around the player. Rule: keep the Visitor's face, the headline and the key object out of the bottom-right area (roughly the last 15% of the width and 10% of the height; this size comes from tool blogs, not YouTube) and away from all four corners. Most viewers browse with a dark interface around the thumbnail (an inference; YouTube publishes no theme share). An almost all-dark image such as one lantern in a black lane can blend into a dark feed, so keep at least one large bright, warm focal area and strong outline contrast. Pixel tables of 'display sizes' (116x65 mobile, 168x94 sidebar and so on) come from tool vendors, not YouTube. Treat them only as a rough reason to test at small sizes.

- **On the mobile home feed, the first seconds of the video can replace the thumbnail as a muted, captioned preview, so the cold open is part of the pitch.** (moderate) With 'Playback in feeds' on (an app setting with Always on, Wi-Fi only and Off options), the YouTube apps start a muted, captioned version of the top video in the home feed about a second after the thumbnail appears. Implication: the opening shot should continue the thumbnail's promise visually (the same Visitor emotion, the same situation or a clear lead into it), and it should work silently, with clean captions and no slow logo or title card. Then the person who stopped for the thumbnail sees the promise confirmed straight away. The evidence is moderate: the mechanism is a known YouTube feature, but the sources are tech press, not current YouTube documentation.

- **TV is now a primary screen for long-form YouTube, and TV sessions run longer.** (moderate) YouTube says viewers watch over a billion hours of YouTube on TVs every day, and that 'viewers watch for longer sessions on TV compared to other devices'. Press reports of YouTube's 2025 announcements say TV passed mobile in US watch time. For a long-form explainer aimed at adults aged 18-45, a large share of impressions may be on TV, where the thumbnail is shown poster-sized and picked by remote. That favours one bold, clear image over fine detail, but the image also has to look hand-made and polished at full size.

- **Title and thumbnail are one package. YouTube asks for accurate, front-loaded titles, and thumbnail text should add to the title rather than repeat it.** (moderate) YouTube's title guidance: 'Be accurate', 'Be succinct. Viewers may only see part of your title', put important words first, and 'Limit ALL CAPS and emoji'. YouTube describes two kinds of title, searchable and intriguing. The fixed title for episode 001 is intriguing and about 55 characters, so its end ('All Day?') may be cut on some surfaces. The thumbnail therefore has to show 'medieval London, ordinary daily life' visually, through the period street or interior with the modern Visitor in it as contrast. It should not spend headline space on words the title already gives ('MEDIEVAL', 'LONDON', 'ALL DAY'). The headline should add a specific, true, surprising angle that answers or sharpens the title's question. 'Complement, don't repeat' is practitioner consensus repeated by tool blogs, not a published YouTube rule.

### Measured data
- **No single design feature is a big lever. The idea and the promise matter more than styling tweaks.** (moderate) Eden.so compared 2,960 thumbnails drawn from a 507,478-video corpus (8,691 channels, Aug 2025 to Aug 2026). 'Breakout' meant at least 2x the channel baseline; 'underperformer' meant at or below it. Every single thumbnail feature differed between the two groups by 4.1 percentage points or less. The authors treat gaps under 3 points as noise from the vision model that tagged the images. Content factors were much bigger: video length spread breakout rates by 14.2 points (26.9% under 5 min, 41.1% over 40 min) and title length by 9.8 points. Thumbnail Bench (11,223 covers, 1,043 channels with 100K+ subscribers; outcome was views per day, not CTR) found that its top-viewed covers rarely differed from the rest by more than 2-3 points. For Life, Previously: put the effort into one strong, true, specific image idea. Fine-tuning saturation or layout comes second.  
  Sources: What makes a good YouTube thumbnail? We analyzed 507,478 videos (Eden) - https://eden.so/blog/what-makes-a-good-youtube-thumbnail/; Thumbnail Bench study of 1,043 channels (via NetInfluencer) - https://www.netinfluencer.com/?p=53558
- **YouTube scores thumbnails on watch time, not clicks, so the thumbnail must promise what the video delivers.** (strong) Test & Compare takes up to 3 thumbnails, shows them evenly and picks a winner by watch time share. It does not use CTR, so viewers don't waste time clicking the wrong videos. Possible results are Winner, Performed Same or Inconclusive. A test runs from a few days up to 2 weeks. Variants that are too similar make the test run longer. YouTube's CTR FAQ says clickbait gives high CTR but low view duration, which cuts recommendations. Creator Liaison Rene Ritchie: watch time is returned because it 'includes both' CTR and retention. For episode 001: the thumbnail should sell the title's 'all day' promise and hint at the opening scenes. Then run 2-3 clearly different concepts in Test & Compare, not small colour variants. It is desktop Studio only, and not available for premieres.  
  Sources: Test & compare thumbnails (YouTube Help) - https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13861714; Impressions and click-through rate FAQ (YouTube Help) - https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154?hl=en-GB; Do Faces Help YouTube Thumbnails? Here's What The Data Says (Search Engine Journal, quoting Rene Ritchie) - https://www.searchenginejournal.com/do-faces-help-youtube-thumbnails-heres-what-the-data-says/563944/
- **Judge CTR against realistic baselines. On a new channel, early CTR is noisy.** (strong) YouTube says half of all channels and videos have an impressions CTR between 2% and 10%. CTR naturally falls as a video gets more homepage and browse impressions, and channel-page traffic inflates it. New channels (under a week old) and videos under 100 views can swing even more. For a zero-subscriber launch: don't swap thumbnails because of first-day CTR. Use a Test & Compare result, or wait until impressions reach the thousands.  
  Sources: Impressions and click-through rate FAQ (YouTube Help) - https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154?hl=en-GB

No text: 13.4% vs 16.8% (-3.4). 7+ words: 29.6% vs 31.1% (-1.5). 1-3 words: +0.8, no advantage. Repeating the title word for word made no difference (6.3% vs 6.8%). About 39% of thumbnails in both groups share no words with the title. All caps: 62.3% vs 59.7% (+2.6, noise). Thumbnail Bench: median 4 words; its top covers repeated the title slightly less often (33% vs 35%). vidIQ's top-50 breakouts: median 5 words. For episode 001: the title already asks the question, so the overlay should be a short, concrete, true hook that the title doesn't contain. It should not restate 'medieval London all day'.

- **A face on its own adds little. The studies disagree, and the ones with control groups find almost no effect.** (moderate) Eden: any human face 84.2% vs 82.2% (+2.0, within noise); exactly one face +1.5. 1of10 Media (300,000+ outlier videos from 2025, via SEJ): thumbnails with and without faces 'perform similarly'. ### Professional craft
- **The title and thumbnail are one unit. The thumbnail has to sell the promise of the whole title, not a single segment of the video.** (moderate) YouTube says titles and thumbnails exist to 'help a viewer understand what the video is about', and its A/B tool now tests title and thumbnail together (title only, thumbnail only, or both). The owner's rejected concepts fail on this point: 'AFTER DARK?' sells only the curfew, and 'YOUR BEDMATES' sells only waking up. Before drawing anything, ask: if someone saw only this image beside the title 'What Did People in Medieval London Actually Do All Day?', would they expect a tour of a working day? The word that carries the title is 'Actually', the gap between what people assume and what was true. So the image should show one concrete, surprising daytime activity that stands for 'this is what a normal day really involved', e.g. work, buying food, or the city's rules, rather than a night scene or an abstract dawn-to-curfew diagram. Write down the one-sentence promise first ('a normal day in 1390s London was stranger and more regulated than you think'), then judge every concept against that sentence.

- **Thumbnail text must add something the title doesn't say. Never repeat the title.** (moderate) The thumbnail opens the curiosity gap and the title gives the context. They should not duplicate each other. In Eden's 507,478-video corpus, repeating the title word for word gave no advantage: 6.3% of breakouts vs 6.8% of underperformers. Thumbnails with no overlap with the title were about the same in both groups (39.2% vs 39.1%). So repetition wastes the pixels; it doesn't actively hurt. For this title, avoid 'ALL DAY', 'MEDIEVAL LONDON' or 'WHAT DID THEY DO'. Use the text for a stake or a twist the title lacks: a number, a rule, a consequence or a reaction (e.g. a short claim about the job or rule shown in the image). Every word must be backed by claims.md.

### Illustrated / history explainers
- **Design for watch-time share, not raw CTR. The thumbnail is a promise, and it has to promise the whole video the title names.** (strong) YouTube's own Test & Compare picks winners by watch-time share, not CTR. YouTube says over-indexing on CTR 'could become click-bait', which hurts retention and recommendations. For Ep 001 this means the image must promise what the title promises: a whole ordinary day, mostly work, food, rules and filth. A single later moment such as the curfew is not enough, which is why the owner rejected 'AFTER DARK?'. The best visuals stand for the whole day.
