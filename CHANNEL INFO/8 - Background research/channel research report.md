## 2. How the channel works

### Its audience promise

The channel describes itself as exploring human history, behavior, and everyday science. Its catalog currently emphasizes prehistoric life. The appealing questions are practical and imaginable: what people did with their time, how they handled weather, how they traveled, and what made human life possible.

The viewer does not need an existing interest in archaeology to understand being cold, bored, hungry, tired, or short of time. **My interpretation:** familiar human concerns provide the entry point; historical explanation supplies the surprise.

### Its packaging

The title supplies a question. The thumbnail simplifies that question into an emotion, predicament, or provocative implication. The strongest example is **"What Did Ancient Humans Actually Do All Day?"** paired with **"NO JOBS"** and a visibly relaxed character. It evokes the viewer's relationship with work before the episode begins.

Sampled thumbnails use large expressive faces, readable silhouettes, illustrated environments, and prominent yellow lettering with dark outlines. Those specific combinations are part of the reference's recognizable identity. Our thumbnails need their own compositions and visual grammar.

Direct address puts the viewer inside the scene. Modern comparisons add humor. One solved problem often exposes the next.

| A simple recurring illustrated identity | A different silhouette, costume, palette, setting, and movement vocabulary. |

Changing a shirt color or replacing words in an existing script would leave most of the creative work borrowed. A useful originality test is: **if both channels covered the same broad subject, would their central question, argument, scenes, jokes, thumbnail, and ending still be recognizably different?**

## 5. Three directions to explore

These are proposals, not final names or claims of market validation.

### A. History: The Supply Room

**Promise:** how ordinary people solved the practical problems that kept society running.

**Character:** Mara, a resourceful quartermaster. She notices supplies, labor, and overlooked workers. Her instinct is to ask who had to make an impressive historical achievement work. She improvises, runs into constraints, and learns to respect the people behind a solution.

**Look:** a stocky trapezoidal human silhouette, geometric hair, oversized pockets; emerald, plum, and pale pink. Miniature cutaway buildings and moving supply routes unfold on a workbench. Thumbnails emphasize one large object and its practical problem.

**Possible pilots:** "How Ice Became a Business Before Electricity"; "Who Fed a City Under Siege?"; "How News Travelled Faster Than Its Messenger." Each would need a specific documented case before scripting.

**Tradeoff:** strong visual stories and a clear identity, with more historical research and scene-building effort.

### B. Behavior: The Tiny Decisions Office

**Promise:** small experiments that reveal why ordinary choices become unexpectedly complicated.

**Character:** Nib, an expressive folded-envelope clerk. Nib wants everyone to make sensible decisions, then discovers the same habits in themself. The humor comes from failed predictions and honest revisions.

**Look:** a rectangular body, mitten hands, movable eyebrows; violet, coral, and turquoise. A surreal office becomes a laboratory. Doors represent options; trays hold unfinished tasks; furniture changes when the experiment changes.

**Possible pilots:** "Why Nobody Wants to Go First"; "When More Choices Make Choosing Harder"; "Why an Unfinished Task Follows You Home." Titles remain hypotheses until the evidence supports the promised explanation.

**Tradeoff:** reusable settings and immediate relevance, but the research must distinguish context-dependent findings from universal rules.

### C. Bridge: We Made This Weird

**Promise:** the everyday rules, objects, and systems humans invented to live together—and the behavior that follows.

**Character:** Mox, a squat, square-eared raccoon with an oversized mint satchel. Curious, observant, and mildly mischievous. Mox asks what problem an arrangement was supposed to solve, tries an appealing shortcut, and discovers its consequences. People are interesting, not the target of contempt.

**Look:** compact paper-puppet forms, textured miniature scenery, plum/mint/coral, and a recurring model city. Tickets, receipts, fences, and clocks become physical story devices. Thumbnails show one familiar object behaving unexpectedly, with little text.

**Possible pilots:** "How the Clock Became Everyone's Boss"; "Why We Invented Receipts"; "How a Queue Becomes a Society." Historical origins and behavioral mechanisms require separate evidence; visual similarity across eras cannot establish a causal connection.

**Tradeoff:** room for both interests under one promise, provided each episode stays focused on a particular human arrangement. This is my preferred concept to develop first, while keeping A and B in the comparison.

## 6. Make the character part of the story

For the bridge concept, a repeatable episode could follow this structure:

1. **A visible puzzle:** Mox encounters an ordinary arrangement that seems unnecessarily complicated.
2. **A confident prediction:** Mox proposes a simpler solution; the audience can predict its result.
3. **A concrete failure:** the shortcut breaks something viewers can see.
4. **An investigation:** one documented case or experiment explains the missing constraint.
5. **A model:** objects demonstrate how the solution works and who bears its costs.
6. **A revised understanding:** Mox returns to the opening situation with a better explanation and one remaining question.

For example, an original opening for a queue episode could begin with Mox replacing a long line with a sign reading "Just take turns." Everyone immediately thinks it is their turn. The opening establishes character, conflict, and a question before the explanation starts. This is a fictional setup, not a scientific finding.

The character needs an understandable desire, a recurring mistake, and a way of learning. That is a stronger foundation than a mascot placed beside generic narration.

## 7. A production system we can actually use

**Start with a pilot, then measure the workload.** A provisional 6–9 minute target is a planning choice, not an algorithm rule or a promise about earnings.

| Stage | Concrete output | Completion check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Topic and packaging | One-sentence question; three title/thumbnail pairings | A viewer can understand the promise without an explanation. |
| Research | Claim sheet with sources, dates, scope, and uncertainty | Major factual claims have support; the conclusion does not outrun it. |
| Script | Original argument, character actions, opening, and payoff | Every section advances the question or changes the situation. |
| Voice | Approved narration using an original or appropriately licensed voice | Names, pronunciations, pauses, and timing work when heard aloud. |
| Storyboard | Scene IDs, narration ranges, visual purpose, assets, and movement notes | The visuals explain, demonstrate, or add humor. |
| Asset production | Character turnarounds, expression set, props, backgrounds | Faces, proportions, costume, palette, and period details remain consistent. |
| Edit | Narration-led rough cut, purposeful movement, music and effects | Text is readable; audio is balanced; no slide is held merely to fill time. |
| Review | Factual check, originality pass, mobile thumbnail check, complete playback | The title's promise is delivered clearly and honestly. |

A text model can help research organization and drafts; narration, illustration, and editing tools can follow the roles shown in the tutorial. We should choose specific tools after a small visual and voice test, rather than assume the demonstrated stack is necessary. No paid generation or publishing was performed for this research.

A particularly useful distinction for our writing is **evidence / reconstruction / interpretation**. Name the population, period, and situation when a finding is narrow. Let the character admit uncertainty. Do not turn a catchy ending into a stronger claim than the research supports.

## 8. How to choose without committing too early

First develop **one 45-second opening, one thumbnail composition, and one character sheet for each direction**. These are concept tests, not three complete productions. Compare clarity, memorability, visual storytelling possibilities, and the effort required to make the next ten episodes.

Then select one show identity and build two pilots within it: one history-heavy and one behavior-heavy, connected by the same audience promise. Keep their length and production effort reasonably comparable. Measure actual hours and generation revisions during production.

If published, assess impressions and click-through by traffic source, first-30-second retention, where viewers leave, average viewing duration, returning viewers, and comments that describe what people valued. Use our own comparable uploads as the baseline; there is no single universal target that proves success. Small samples and unequal exposure require patience.

**What success would look like at this stage:** viewers remember the character, understand the channel's promise, and ask for another episode of this particular show. That is the basis for a channel with its own identity.
