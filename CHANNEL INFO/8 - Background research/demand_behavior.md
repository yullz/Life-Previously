# Research: demand_behavior

Collected 5 Oct 2026 by an independent research agent. Confidence labels are the agent's own.

## Headline
Concept B as written (office-lab episodes built on named findings) has the weakest evidence of the three. The closest existing videos to all three pilots draw between 5 views and about 20K, and two of the three pilots rest on findings that meta-analyses do not support as popularly told. The illustrated-psychology category is also weak: incumbents earn under 1% of their subscriber count per upload, and 2025 AI-assisted entrants have fallen 100 to 1000 times from their hits. Behavior content still produces outliers as concrete curiosity questions or inside the "ancient humans + daily need" frame, but that frame is a cloned title lottery that WIRED has publicly labelled "Stone Age Slop".

### F5 [medium] (c) Zeigarnik effect: the classic memory claim is not supported; pilot 3 needs heavy caveats or a rebuild around resumption and rumination evidence.
Ghibellini & Meier 2025 meta-analysis (Humanities and Social Sciences Communications): no memory advantage for unfinished tasks; a resumption tendency (Ovsiankina effect) holds. Search snippet reports recall ratio ~0.99 and ~67% resumption (full text not retrieved). Van Bergen 1968 also failed to replicate. Better-supported neighbours: Syrek et al., unfinished tasks predicted worse weekend sleep via rumination (59 employees); Scullin 2018, bedtime to-do lists sped sleep onset ~9 minutes.

### F6 [medium] (c) Choice overload: conditional, with an average effect near zero; present only as "sometimes, under these conditions", and note that too few options is the more commonly reported problem.
Scheibehenne et al. 2010: 50 experiments, N=5,036, mean effect virtually zero with large variance. Chernev et al. 2015: 99 observations; overload appears under four moderators (task difficulty, choice-set complexity, preference uncertainty, effort-minimizing goal). Reutskaja et al. six-nation survey: people reported too few options 51% of the time versus too many 14%. The jam-study anecdote alone is not a safe basis.

### F7 [medium] (c) Bystander effect and pluralistic ignorance: the safest of the three if framed accurately. The lab effect is real but moderate, real-world intervention is the norm, and the Kitty Genovese "38 witnesses" story must not be used as fact.
Fischer et al. 2011: 105 effect sizes, >7,700 participants, g = -0.35, weaker in dangerous emergencies. Philpot et al. 2020: CCTV of 219 public conflicts in Amsterdam, Cape Town, Lancaster; at least one bystander intervened in 91%, and more bystanders meant more help. Manning et al. 2007: no evidence for 38 passive Genovese witnesses. 2026 Nature Climate Change (N=5,081): misperceptions run both ways; corrections didn't change real donations.

## Implications
1. Do not launch Concept B as a standalone brand, and do not open with its three pilots as titled. None of the framings checked shows demand from small channels in the past year, and the category's incumbents and recent entrants are both weak.
2. If behavior episodes are kept, rebuild the pilots on what the evidence supports. Pilot 1: "would strangers actually help you?", built on the 219-incident CCTV study and the Genovese myth. Pilot 2: explicitly conditional, including that people report too few options more often than too many. Pilot 3: drop the Zeigarnik memory claim and the waiter anecdote, and use the resumption and rumination/sleep studies, or cut it.
3. Packaging rule: keep named textbook effects out of titles and thumbnails. Ask a concrete question about a universal experience (sleep, night, privacy, embarrassment, boredom) and introduce the term inside the video. That is where rigor-compatible outliers appear (497K in 8 days, 554K), while named explainers sit at 5-20K.
4. Concept C's direction (behavior inside an everyday-life frame) matches where demand is, but its proposed objects and systems topics (clock, receipts, queue) do not. Reweight its pilot list toward bodily and daily-need questions, and treat the systems topics as experiments, not launch episodes.
5. Do not copy the "What did ancient humans do..." title pattern. At least six channels are reusing identical titles, a clone with one 909K hit gets 170-14K on everything else, and WIRED's "Stone Age Slop" fact-check has 1.49M views. On-screen sourcing and an evidence grade per claim are a real differentiator here.
6. Plan for power-law outcomes and short trend life. The "psychology of people who" originators fell from millions to 2-12K per upload within about 9 months. Budget 20-30 episodes, judge the channel by median views and returning viewers, and avoid a daily-upload template that depends on one title pattern.
7. Avoid the top-clicking but unscientific framings: "psychology of people who" trait profiles, Gen X or generational profiling, dark psychology and Machiavelli, and manipulation-framed uses of Zeigarnik. They conflict with a sources-linked positioning, and most of the originator channels checked are already decaying.
8. Add a free pre-production gate for every title: search YouTube filtered to the past year and sorted by views, and require at least two unrelated small channels above ~100K on the same framing before scripting. This check alone would have flagged all three B pilots and the queue episode.
9. Add a red-flag list to the research claim sheet: Genovese 38 witnesses, the jam study as a universal law, the Zeigarnik waiter story, universal two-shift sleep, the watching-eyes effect, and Calhoun's mouse utopia applied to humans. Each needs an explicit "contested" or "narrow" label in narration.
10. Do not build revenue expectations on psychology RPM claims. Only undisclosed-method estimates of $4-25 exist, so treat monetization as unknown until the channel's own analytics exist.

## Checks of Astra 6 claims
- [partly] IKEA Effect (~16K views): one older psychology upload is insufficient evidence to reject behavior as a niche. -- Fair for a single video, but the wider data points the same way. Ink's four non-prehistory uploads have a median of ~99K versus ~588K for twelve prehistory titles. Across channels, Sprouts' Paradox of Choice has 19.6K, Zenn's Marshmallow Effect 18K, and dozens of named-effect explainers are under 500 views. Behavior is not dead, but named-finding packaging is weak.
- [partly] Concept B tradeoff: reusable settings and immediate relevance, but research must distinguish context-dependent findings from universal rules. -- The caveat is right but understated. The Zeigarnik memory effect is null in a 2025 meta-analysis, choice overload averages about zero, and the bystander effect's canonical Genovese story is a myth. "Immediate relevance" does not show up as demand: the closest existing videos have 5-400 views.
- [partly] Concept B pilots: Why Nobody Wants to Go First; When More Choices Make Choosing Harder; Why an Unfinished Task Follows You Home (titles remain hypotheses). -- Pilot 1 is defensible if rebuilt on CCTV evidence and pluralistic ignorance. Pilot 2's "When" wording is appropriately conditional. Pilot 3 is supportable only through rumination and sleep studies, not the Zeigarnik effect. No demand evidence was found for any of the three.
- [unverifiable] Preferred concept C (rules, objects, systems) with pilots on clocks, receipts and queues. -- Astra says these are not market-validated, and I found no validation either. Queue-history videos have 5-37 views, weekend and 8-hour-day histories 11-5K, and a 1.03M-subscriber everyday-things channel gets about 5-17K per video. The demand that is demonstrated is narrower: ancient humans plus bodily or daily needs.
- [partly] Interpretation: familiar human concerns provide the entry point and historical explanation supplies the surprise; this explains Ink Explainer's appeal. -- Consistent with the cross-channel pattern, but Astra missed that Ink is a follower in a cloned title trend. Zenn's "What Did Ancient Humans Do All Day?" (440K, ~4 months old) and "...at Night?" (8.0M, ~5 months) predate Ink's versions, and several channels reuse identical titles. Zenn's dates are relative labels, not exact timestamps.
- [confirmed] Channel describes itself as history, behavior and everyday science; catalog currently emphasizes prehistoric life. -- 13 of 17 titles are ancient or early-human topics (feed fetched 5 Oct 2026). The four others are IKEA Effect, Life Speeds Up, Animals/Baby and Guns.
- [confirmed] Displayed views: All Day 10,407,278; Rain ~1.5M; Winter ~812K. -- RSS feed on 5 Oct 2026 shows 10,544,127, 1,586,545 and 822,041, consistent with one more day of growth.

## Gaps
- No first-party RPM, CTR or retention data for any channel. RPM figures are third-party estimates with undisclosed methods.
- YouTube data is a single snapshot (5 Oct 2026, US/English results) pulled from public search and channel HTML by script. Channel-tab counts are rounded, and search rankings may vary by region and session.
- Trend comparisons (newest 10 versus older uploads) are confounded by video age. Decline is firm for Sprouts and for the boom-bust entrants, weaker for Psych2Go and School of Life, whose newest uploads are currently above their 3-6 month median.
- Low views on tiny channels reflect missing distribution as well as topic demand. The absence of a recent hit shows there is no proof of demand, not that demand is impossible.
- AI-assisted production of the newer channels is inferred from upload cadence and style. I did not watch the videos or verify their toolchains.
- The science was verified through abstracts, repository pages and search summaries, not full texts. The Ghibellini & Meier figures (recall ratio ~0.99, ~67% resumption, 59 publications) come from a search snippet, because Nature redirected to a login page.
- Not checked: replication status of Masicampo & Baumeister 2011 (plan-making), the IKEA effect, and Scullin 2018 (small sample).
- I could not confirm the content or accuracy of Zenn's "The Slotow Effect" or "The Minnesota Effect", or whether Dumanity's format is comparable. Titles and views only.
- Exact publish dates for Zenn's videos were not retrieved, so the claim that Zenn predates Ink rests on YouTube's relative age labels.
- YouTube's monetization policy on mass-produced or inauthentic content was not researched here and may affect any AI-assisted format.
