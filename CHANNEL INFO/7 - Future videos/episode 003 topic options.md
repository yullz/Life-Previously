## 1. Did Ancient Egyptian Workers Have Better Jobs Than Us?

**Recommended for episode 003.**

**Thumbnail:** **PAID SICK DAYS?** A worker resting at home, grain rations as the concrete benefit, and the Visitor's surprised reaction. One clear foreground idea, warm Egyptian colours, the channel's orange place-and-year chip. No modern money, generic pyramids or fabricated readable hieroglyphs.

**Why someone might click:** It makes ancient history relevant to the viewer's own job. The apparent contradiction—ancient workers receiving a benefit many viewers worry about—creates a specific question.

**Story:** Follow a royal tomb worker at Deir el-Medina through work, provisions, absence records and household obligations. Compare documented advantages with the physical demands and dependence on the state. The ending answers whether this particular job was actually better, using explicit dimensions rather than a sweeping verdict.

**Historical foundation:** Research on Deir el-Medina supports paid sick leave and organised care within this particular community. See [Stanford's presentation of Anne Austin's research](https://classics.stanford.edu/events/fight-survive-ancient-egypt-health-care-and-disease-deir-el-medina) and [Austin's UCLA dissertation](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4rw1m0cz).

**Essential boundary:** These were a particular workforce of royal tomb builders, not all ancient Egyptians or the builders of the Giza pyramids. Payments and provisions must be explained in their own historical terms. Fix the episode's exact year after identifying the records we will use; later labour disputes cannot be presented as the same day's events.

**Production fit:** Strong. A limited set of homes, workplace environments and evidence cards can support the story with deliberate reuse. Recurring sets still need accurate Egyptian references and full-size acceptance checks.

## 2. The Winter That Froze Europe: How Did People Survive?

**Thumbnail:** **BREAD FROZE SOLID** A frozen loaf in the foreground, the shivering Visitor and a recognisable period home in cold blue tones. Final bread detail and visual treatment require source confirmation before illustration.

**Why someone might click:** An extreme, imaginable problem: how do you eat and stay warm when ordinary household supplies freeze? The survival question offers broad appeal, and an autumn or winter release is a plausible seasonal fit, not a measured demand result.

**Story:** Paris, January 1709. Follow an ordinary household trying to obtain fuel, water and food. Connect the immediate cold to disrupted transport and the later food crisis, with explicit time transitions.

**Historical foundation:** [Météo-France's interview with historian Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie](https://meteofrance.com/meteo-a-z/le-grand-hiver-1709) discusses exceptional cold, surviving measurements and the social context. [The Making of Revolutionary Paris](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.1525/j.ctt1png4x), chapter two, describes bread requiring an axe indoors; check the underlying citation before using that image as the episode's promise.

**Essential boundary:** Avoid unsupported “coldest ever” claims, a single temperature applied to all Europe, or portraying one Paris household as every person's experience. The food crisis developed over time; do not collapse the winter and later shortages into one day.

**Production fit:** Moderate. Cold interiors, clothing layers and lighting will need a small representative visual test. Several location changes can be made from approved base scenes.

## 3. How Did the Aztecs Feed a City Built on a Lake?

**Thumbnail:** **FARMS IN A LAKE?** One large, clearly readable raised field surrounded by water, a loaded canoe and the Visitor's astonishment. Keep the city distant and simple so the thumbnail remains readable on a phone.

**Why someone might click:** The image poses an immediate puzzle: a large city surrounded by water still needs breakfast. It offers the “people in the past solved problems cleverly” appeal in the owner's example.

**Story:** Tenochtitlan and its supply region in 1519. Follow a meal from cultivation and transport to market and household. Explain how farming, trade and tribute worked together, then show where the system was vulnerable.

**Historical foundation:** [UNAM's account of Mexica economic life](https://e1.portalacademico.cch.unam.mx/alumno/historiademexico1/unidad2/culturamexica/economia) explains that chinampas supported the city but did not supply all its needs. [UNESCO's description of Mexico City and Xochimilco](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/412) provides the landscape and agricultural context.

**Essential boundary:** Chinampas are built-up agricultural plots, not drifting rafts. Do not claim the Aztecs invented the entire technique, that the city was completely self-sufficient, or that it was universally better than modern farming.

**Production fit:** The highest visual risk of these three. Water levels, causeways, canoes, crops and architecture must remain consistent. A simple accurate diagram may explain the system more reliably than a complex generated aerial view.
