## Myths to correct (say how sure we are)
- **Lava buried Pompeii.** No lava reached it: about eighteen hours of pumice and ash fall, then pyroclastic surges. Certain. [C10]
- **Everyone died, frozen in place.** About thirteen hundred people's remains have been found; most residents probably left during the pumice fall, though how many died fleeing is unknown. High for the counts. [C11]
- **The casts are bodies.** They are plaster poured into hollows, often with bones inside; DNA has overturned some of the stories told about them. Certain. [C12]
- **The eruption was on 24 August (or "it's now proven it was 24 October").** The date is open. [C13]
- **Pliny described Pompeii's destruction.** He never mentions Pompeii. Certain. [C14]
- **Fullers collected urine in pots on street corners.** Fullers used urine, but the street-pot story rests on weak evidence. [C38]
- **Everyone wiped with a shared sponge on a stick.** One text (Seneca) mentions one in a latrine; how Pompeians wiped is unknown and some think it cleaned the toilet. Medium. [C46]
- **Ordinary people lived on scraps.** Drains in a modest district held a varied diet. Sure for that district; don't generalise. [C37]
- **Graffiti were vandalism.** They were normal, inside rich homes as well as outside. [C25]
- **Pompeii had dozens of brothels.** Only one is certain. (Not planned for this episode.) [C55]

## Contested points (say the uncertainty aloud)
- Population: under ten thousand to twenty thousand or more; the Park says at least 20,000 (2025). Never one figure. [C1]
- The day of the eruption: open (Zuchtriegel et al. 2024). [C13]
- Literacy: unknown; Harris (1989) about ten percent across the Roman world; others argue many more had basic reading. [C27]
- Number of bars: about eighty (Park) to about a hundred and sixty (Ellis), depending on the definition. [C28]
- Share enslaved: one recent model, a fifth to nearly a third. [C40]
- Odd endorsements ("late-night drinkers", "little thieves"): joke, real fans or a smear; we can't tell. [C18]
- Julia Felix's "nongentum": meaning unknown; not "900 rooms". [C20]
- Whether the ten-year ban on shows was lifted early: no solid source; don't say either way.

## Behavior (why they did it, in one sentence)
The wall was the one place the whole street would see, and writing on it was normal, not vandalism, so anyone with a room to let, a candidate to push, wine to sell or a grudge to settle put it on the wall. [C25, C3, C16, C33]

## Hook
- Stakes for the viewer: drop into a town where every wall talks (rents, elections, prices, insults, love notes). Read the walls and you know how to get through the day; and the people writing them sound exactly like us.
- Viewer's objection: "Isn't Pompeii just the town buried by lava, where everyone froze on the spot?" (Answered at the end: no lava, and most people probably got out.)
- Questions for the cold open:
  1. Where do you get water when your room has no tap?
  2. What does a cup of wine cost?
  3. **Held to the end:** the day the volcano erupted, the date everyone "knows": is it even right?

## Proposed sections (cold open, seven sections, payoff; about 1,700 words)
1. **Cold open.** You wake in a loft above a shop, AD 79. Every wall outside is covered in writing. You're thirsty and there's no tap.
2. **Water and streets.** The corner fountain, water towers, overflow in the gutter, stepping stones, cart ruts; parts of town are still a building site after the earthquake of 62 or 63. [C4-C8]
3. **A room to rent.** Living over the shop; "to let" notices; Julia Felix's lease (evidence moment); women owning property, Eumachia and the fullers. [C3, C20, C41]
4. **Breakfast at the counter.** Eighty to a hundred and sixty bars; the Regio V counter; Hedone's prices (evidence moment); bakeries and the prison mill; the myth that ordinary people ate scraps. [C28-C34, C37]
5. **Work and money.** The fullery and the urine myth; wool tallies; the spending list and "bread for the boy"; slavery; small change. [C35, C36, C38-C40]
6. **Election season.** Posters painted by lamplight; "all the fullers", "the late-night drinkers"; women backing candidates although only male citizens voted; who could read. [C16-C19, C27]
7. **Baths, toilets and gossip.** Separate women's suites; the toilet next to the kitchen; the sponge myth; "Crapper, beware"; insults and a love triangle; children's drawings. [C22-C26, C42-C47]
8. **The games.** The amphitheatre; the riot of AD 59 and the ten-year ban; Celadus, the girls' heartthrob. [C21, C48-C53]
9. **Payoff.** Pliny's letters; two stages, no lava; about thirteen hundred found, most probably fled; the date is open. Back to the opening wall: "I marvel, wall, that you haven't collapsed..." It didn't. It's still talking. [C9-C14, C22]

## C41 date clarification — director check, 2026-10-08

For P03/S033, retain: "Earlier in the first century A.D., a public priestess called Eumachia paid for a grand building on the Forum with her own money."

The date of the building is supported by the factual "THE BUILDING IN THE FORUM" section of the [Archaeological Park's Eumachia page](https://pompeiisites.org/en/exhibitions-and-events/eumachia-en/), read directly on 2026-10-08. It dates construction to AD 2-3 and identifies her private funding and public priesthood. The existing C41 already dated Eumachia's life; this addition makes the connection to the building explicit. It does not rely on the page's fictional first-person presentation.

Attempt to refute: the [research record for CIL X 810/811](https://heurist.huma-num.fr/heurist/judaism_and_rome/tpl/public-record/294), also read directly, uses a wider 27 BCE–43 CE metadata range and discusses competing interpretations of the dedication and her son's identity. Thus the precise construction year is not settled. The director retains the Park-supported broad early-AD chronology for this episode, without asserting an exact year, her death date, or an AD 79 construction. The inscription independently supports the priesthood and private funding. This is a source-based editorial dating choice, not a claim of scholarly unanimity.
