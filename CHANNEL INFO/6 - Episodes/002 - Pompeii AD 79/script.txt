## Water on every corner
::: S013 | [reuse S010] [zoom-in]
Here's your first surprise. Most homes in Pompeii have no piped water at all.
::: S014 | A simple Roman room with plastered walls, a woman carrying a heavy clay water jug on her shoulder in through the doorway, an empty floor on the right [pose: look-around 0.73,0.97 0.69]
Someone in the house fetches it from the corner fountain, or draws rainwater from a cistern.
::: S015 | Map: a simple illustrated plan of a Roman town seen straight from above, plain with no labels: an irregular oval ring wall, an older core of skewed blocks in the lower left and straighter blocks elsewhere, and more than forty small blue dots for fountains, each dot at a crossroads between the blocks [still]
There are at least forty street fountains across town. For most people, one is just a short walk away.
::: S016 | [reuse S010] [pose: splash-face 0.74,0.97 0.55] [sfx: water-splash]

::: S019 | A Roman street with overflow water from a fountain running down the grey stone paving between two high raised pavements, three large oval stepping stones crossing the road [zoom-in]
All that overflow runs down the street. Which explains these.
::: S020 | [reuse S019] [pose: tiptoe 0.53,0.75 0.25]
Pavements are high, and big stepping stones let you cross with dry feet.
::: S021 | Object insert: two deep parallel ruts worn by cart wheels into large grey paving blocks, filling the frame
Cart wheels have worn deep ruts into the stone. And some streets are one-way.
::: S022 | A narrow Roman street with a mule cart waiting at a corner, the man driving it shrugging, an empty stretch of pavement on the right [pose: eyebrow-raise 0.89,0.97 0.6] [still]

::: S023 | A Roman house front under repair: wooden scaffolding, two plasterers smoothing fresh white plaster on the wall, broken roof tiles and a trench with a lead pipe dug up in the street [pan-right]

::: S024 | A Roman street just after an earthquake: cracked walls, fallen roof tiles and a toppled column, townspeople clearing rubble with baskets [still]
In sixty-two or sixty-three A.D., a powerful earthquake wrecked much of Pompeii. Sixteen or seventeen years later, parts of town are still rebuilding.
::: S025 | The upper floor of a Roman building seen from the street: small shuttered windows above a row of shops, a woman shaking out a blanket from one window
So where does everyone actually live?

## A room to let

The better-off live in big atrium houses, often with shops along the front that they rent out.
::: S028 | A painted Roman house front, on its left half a doorway with a flat white lime-wash rectangle beside it, filled with small soft smudged red brush strokes, an empty stretch of pavement on the right [pose: scratch-head 0.90,0.97 0.55 flip]

One landlord offers shops with their lofts, and upstairs flats fit for a knight. For details, ask his slave, Primus.

## Breakfast at the counter
::: S036 | Wide view of a Roman street with several masonry food counters at the street corners, clay jars set into their tops, customers leaning on them [pan-right] [sfx: crowd-roman-street 0.3]
In the ruins today, the archaeological park counts about eighty food counters. One detailed survey finds about a hundred and sixty.
::: S037 | A Roman street-corner food counter whose painted front shows a sea nymph riding a sea-horse, two ducks and a rooster, clay jars set into the counter top, a cook ladling stew, an empty stretch of pavement on the right [pose: watch-arms-crossed right 0.62]
A modern excavation, completed in twenty-twenty, uncovered a counter like this, painted with a sea nymph, ducks and a rooster.
::: S038 | Object insert: clay jars sunk into a masonry counter top, holding stew with pieces of duck, fish and snails in their shells [same-as: S037]
The jars still held scraps of duck, pig, goat or sheep, fish... and snails.
::: S039 | Object insert: a close daylight view of the street end of the same masonry food counter, standing on grey paving stones: a thick pale stone counter top with the rim of a clay pot set into it, the end panel painted cream inside a dark-red border band, a brown dog with a plain collar painted on it in loose flat fresco style on matte plaster, a short scrawl of thin scratched lines along the red border band below the dog, and the edge of the sea-horse panel turning the corner at the left [same-as: S037] [still]
And someone scratched a rude insult on the frame around the painting of a dog.
::: S040 | Inside a Roman bar: a masonry counter with clay jars, wine amphorae stacked against the back wall, a woman serving behind the bar pouring wine into a clay cup, the plastered wall behind covered in small unreadable scratched marks, two local men leaning on the far end of the counter on the right, an empty floor on the left [pose: count-fingers 0.25,0.97 0.74 flip] [sfx: tavern-crowd 0.25]

"Pay four, and you'll drink Falernian." The top of the range.
::: S043 | Object insert: five small copper coins in a row on a worn masonry counter, the first one slid a little apart from the other four
A copper is small change. One list scratched on a wall puts a daily allowance at five coppers. So the cheap wine is a fifth of that.
::: S044 | A Roman bakery: a domed brick oven with a fire glowing inside, round loaves scored into segments cooling on a wooden board, a baker holding a long wooden peel
For bread, there are more than thirty bakeries in town.

Grain is ground in big stone mills, turned by donkeys, or by enslaved workers.

::: S048 | A modest Roman neighbourhood near a town gate: small shops and plain houses along a street, a stone drain opening in the roadway, an empty stretch of pavement on the right [pose: shrug-smirk 0.86,0.97 0.6] [still]

## Election season
::: S062 | A Roman street at midday, the plaster house fronts painted with rows of flat white lime-wash rectangles, each filled with soft smudged red brush strokes, a crowd passing beneath them [pan-left] [sfx: crowd-roman-street 0.4]
Back on the street, the walls are shouting. Most of the painted notices are election adverts. About two and a half thousand survive.
::: S063 | Split composition: on the left a Roman magistrate in a toga inspecting workers relaying the grey stone paving of a road between plastered red and ochre house fronts, the green slope of Vesuvius behind; on the right a magistrate in a toga seated on a folding stool on a raised platform, listening to two townsmen arguing their case
Every year, probably in March, Pompeii elects its magistrates, the men who run the town.
::: S064 | Object insert: a flat patch of fresh white lime-wash on a plastered wall, painted with a few big soft smudged red brush strokes [note: Election notices, Pompeii, by AD 79: O V F = "I ask you to elect him"] [still]
Many notices carry three letters: O, V, F. Short for "I ask you to elect him."
::: S065 | A Roman street at night: a sign painter on a wooden ladder painting big soft smudged red brush strokes onto a flat white lime-wash rectangle on the plaster wall, a helper holding up a small hand lantern with cloudy horn sides and steadying the ladder, a third man whitewashing a patch of wall beside them, a single moon in the sky
Most are probably the work of sign painters, often in teams, and some seem to work at night.
::: S066 | The same night street seen closer: the painter on the ladder, the white lime-wash rectangle beside him half filled with soft smudged red brush strokes, his helper below holding up a small hand lantern with cloudy horn sides in one hand and steadying the ladder with the other, a third man brushing whitewash onto the wall beside them, an empty stretch of pavement on the far right [same-as: S065] [pose: watch-arms-crossed 0.17,0.995 0.8 flip]
One whitewashes the wall. Another holds the lantern, and the ladder.

Next to one notice, someone painted: "Lantern-carrier, hold the ladder."
::: S068 | A lone Roman man, a sign painter, on a ladder against a house wall at night under a single full moon, brush in hand, the wall beside him painted with soft smudged red brush strokes on a white lime-wash rectangle, no one else in the street [zoom-in] [card: alone_by_moon 0.27,0.14 0.42 +3.7]
One painter, Aemilius Celer, signed a notice for the games: "alone, by the moon".
::: S069 | A Roman house front by day with several flat white lime-wash rectangles painted on the plaster, each filled with soft smudged red brush strokes, a group of cloth-workers in plain tunics pointing proudly at one of them, an empty stretch of pavement on the right [pose: count-fingers right 0.6]

## Baths, toilets and gossip
::: S076 | The courtyard of Roman public baths in the afternoon: a colonnaded exercise yard, people arriving with small bundles of clothes, a bath building with low domed roofs [pan-right] [sfx: crowd-roman-street 0.15]
Either way, it's time for a wash. The Roman architect Vitruvius says bathing hours run mainly from noon to evening.
::: S077 | The exterior of an old Roman bath building on a side street, a small separate doorway on the left with a woman going in through it, an empty stretch of pavement on the right [pose: look-around right 0.6]
The Stabian Baths are the oldest public baths in town, about two hundred years old. Women get a smaller, plainer suite, with its own street door.
::: S078 | Cross-section of a Roman bath's hot room that fills the frame edge to edge: above, men bare-chested with linen cloths wrapped at the waist sitting on marble benches in steam; below the raised floor, a shallow empty space held up by rows of knee-high stacks of square tiles, hot air flowing between them from a glowing furnace mouth on the left where one stoker stands outside, and the heat rising through hollow box tiles inside the walls
Hot air from furnaces runs under the floors and inside the walls.
::: S080 | A small Roman kitchen: a raised masonry hearth at table height with a cooking pot over a small fire, and right beside it a narrow alcove with a low masonry bench, a single round seat opening cut into its top over a pit [zoom-in]
And the toilet? At home, many houses have their own: usually a single seat, often right in the kitchen.

::: S082 | Object insert: a natural sea sponge fixed on the end of a wooden stick, standing in a clay pot of water beside a low masonry latrine bench with a round seat opening cut into its top [still]

## The games
::: S092 | A Roman house front with one very wide white lime-wash band painted on the plaster, filled with big soft smudged red brush strokes, local people gathering in front of it excitedly [sfx: crowd-roman-street 0.4]
From the walls, for a start. There are notices for the games.
::: S093 | Split composition: on the left pairs of gladiators fighting in a sandy arena; on the right a big canvas awning stretched over tiers of stone seats in bright sun
One notice, painted years earlier, promised twenty pairs of gladiators, a proper animal hunt and, the real selling point, awnings to keep off the sun.

## The end of the story
::: S103 | Mount Vesuvius on the left of the frame, seen across Pompeii's rooftops on a calm clear day, open sky on the right [pose: closeup-worried 0.70,1.04 0.95] [still]
So, back to the question from the start. When does all of this end?
::: S104 | Object insert: a single loose leaf of old parchment lying on a wooden desk, covered in small faded brown handwriting, blurred and unreadable [note: Pliny the Younger, Letters 6.16 and 6.20, written years after AD 79] [zoom-in]
To work out the date today, we turn to the oldest copies of a letter by the Roman writer Pliny. They give the twenty-fourth of August.
::: S105 | Interior of a dim Roman room: a plastered wall with a short line of faint black charcoal marks at head height on the left half of the wall, blurred and unreadable, a beaten-earth floor with an empty stretch on the right [note: Charcoal note, Pompeii: "16th day before the Kalends of November" (17 October), year unknown] [zoom-in]
But in a modern excavation in twenty-eighteen, a charcoal note turned up on a house wall, dated the seventeenth of October.
::: S106 | [reuse S105] [pose: closeup-thoughtful right 0.95] [still]
So lots of people switched to October. But the note has no year.
::: S107 | The same dim plastered wall seen close: a few fresh, crisp black charcoal squiggles, unreadable, a stick of charcoal resting on a stone ledge below [same-as: S105]
And when park staff wrote on that wall in fresh charcoal, it was still easy to read ten months later.
::: S108 | [reuse S105] [pose: shrug-smirk right 0.6]
So the note could be from seventy-eight A.D., the year before the eruption. Which means we don't know the exact day.

Back in seventy-nine A.D., we have an eyewitness to the eruption. Pliny the Younger, aged seventeen, watched from Misenum, across the bay.
