# Narration: What Did People in Medieval London Actually Do All Day?

About 1546 words, roughly 9 to 10 minutes.

## Direction

- Conversational, like telling a friend a story over a drink. Not an announcer, not a trailer voice.
- Dry humour: say the jokes straight and let them land. Don't sell them.
- Questions should sound genuinely curious, not rhetorical.
- About 150 to 160 words per minute, with a beat of breathing room at each chapter heading.
- Read the text exactly as written. If a line doesn't work in your mouth, flag it rather than changing it.

## Pronunciation

- Southwark: say "Suthuck"
- Edward III: say "Edward the Third"

## Cold open

It's still dark. You're lying on a straw mattress, wedged between three people you have never met. And somewhere outside, a bell is ringing. You reach for your phone. No signal. No time. No alarm. Because this is London, in the year thirteen-ninety, and almost nobody in this city owns a clock. Here, your whole day is decided by two things. Bells, and daylight.

They tell you when to wake up, when to start work, and when to eat. And when you're no longer allowed to be outside. Now you're probably thinking: come on. People just got up when they felt like it, and went home whenever they wanted. Not in this city. London has rules for almost everything, and the people who break them end up in the records. So let's live one ordinary day here. When do you start work? What do you eat, when most homes barely have a kitchen?

And what happens to you if you're still in the street when the last bell rings? We'll find that one out tonight. First, you need to get up.

## Woken by bells

That bell is one of many. London has around a hundred parish churches crammed inside its walls. Each one rings its bells for services through the day. The monasteries start even earlier, with prayers in the middle of the night. So the city wakes to a noisy, overlapping chorus. Everyone else sleeps right through it. You don't. A few big churches do have mechanical clocks. St Paul's Cathedral has had one for about a hundred years.

But a clock in a tower doesn't run your day. Daylight does. You get up when it gets light. In summer, that's very early. In winter, your morning just arrives late. Look around. You're sharing this room with the family, the apprentices, maybe a servant. Privacy is not really a thing yet. Lighting the room costs money. Cheap candles are made of tallow, which is animal fat.

They smoke, and they smell exactly like what they are. Beeswax candles burn clean and bright. They're also far too expensive for you. Getting ready is quick. You splash your face and hands from a basin, and that's your morning routine. Full baths do exist. There are bathhouses across the river, in Southwark. But they have a reputation, and it isn't for cleanliness.

## Breakfast and water

Now, breakfast. Here's the surprise: it isn't really a meal yet. Londoners eat two proper meals a day. Dinner, around late morning, and supper in the early evening. If you do hard physical work, you might grab some bread and a drink before you start. That's it. And that drink is probably ale. Which brings us to a myth you've definitely heard. People in the Middle Ages drank beer all day, because the water would kill them.

It's a great story. It's mostly wrong. London actually pipes in fresh water. The Great Conduit in Cheapside has carried spring water into the city since the twelve-hundreds. It flows through lead pipes from springs outside the city, miles away. People line up with buckets, and professional water carriers haul it to houses for a fee. So yes, people drink water.

But ale is food. It's calories, it fills you up, and weak "small ale" is cheap enough to drink all day. And a lot of it is brewed by women, at home, and sold straight from the door.

## Work: sunrise to sunset

Time to work. Your commute is about four steps, because in London, most people work where they live. The ground floor is the shop. In the morning, the front shutter folds down and becomes your counter. Let's say you're an apprentice. You signed up at around fourteen, for at least seven years. Your master feeds you, houses you, and teaches you the trade. In return, you work, you obey, you stay out of taverns, and you don't get married until you're done.

Your hours are simple. You work while it's light. Long days in summer, short days in winter. And here's the part that surprises people. Many trades weren't allowed to work through the night. In thirteen-forty-five, London's spur makers were ordered to stop work at the curfew bell. The reasons are in the city's records. Nobody, the ordinance says, can work as neatly by night as by day. It also complains that some spur makers wander about all day, get drunk, and then fire up their forges at night.

And a blazing forge at night, in a city full of timber, puts the whole neighbourhood in danger. Quality control and fire safety, in one rule. Your boss can't keep you working all night, because the city won't let him. There's one more thing on every worker's mind. About forty years ago, the Black Death killed a huge share of the population. Workers became scarce. So they asked for more money. The government passed laws to cap wages. And workers kept quietly ignoring them.

## Dinner from a cookshop

Late morning. Your stomach is the clock now. Time for dinner. But here's the problem. Many poorer Londoners rent a single room. No real kitchen, no oven, and firewood costs money. So what do you do? The same thing you'd do today. You get takeaway. London is full of cookshops and street sellers. Hot pies, roast meat, pasties, pea pottage, bread and cheese. Historians have argued that for poor city people, this wasn't a treat. It was the sensible option.

The city watches this food closely. A loaf of bread has a legally fixed weight for its price. A baker caught selling short loaves could be dragged through the streets on a sledge, with the bad loaf hung around his neck. Sell rotten meat, and you could be locked in the pillory while your stinking meat is burned right under your nose. Food safety, medieval style. It works because everyone in the street gets to watch.

## The streets

After dinner, you're sent out on an errand. And the street hits you all at once. Sellers shouting what they've got. Carts, horses, pigs, dogs, and hammers ringing from the workshops. The lanes are narrow. Upper floors jut out over the street, so some alleys barely see the sun. And then there's the waste. You've heard that people just threw it out of the window. London actually had laws against dumping filth in the street, and paid men called rakers to cart the rubbish away.

Did people break those laws? Constantly. That's exactly how we know about them. Need a toilet? There are public latrines, including one on London Bridge that drops straight into the Thames. At home, you probably use a privy over a cesspit. When it's full, men called gong farmers come at night and dig it out. It's one of the worst jobs in the city. It's also one of the best paid.

Privies can be dangerous, too. In thirteen-twenty-six, a man called Richard the Raker fell through the rotten floor of his privy... and drowned in the pit underneath. We only know because the city coroner wrote it down. So, yes. Watch your step.

## Free time

The afternoon fades, and work stops when the light goes. So what do people do for fun? Sundays and church holy days are days off. The Church says no work, and those days add up through the year. People play dice, chess, and ball games. Football is hugely popular. It's also loud, violent, and messy. So messy that in thirteen-fourteen, London banned it, because of the noise and chaos of people chasing large balls through the streets.

Fifty years later, the king had another idea. In thirteen-sixty-three, Edward III ordered men to practise archery on holidays. Instead of playing games. England needed archers. Your free time is military training. And then there's the alehouse. A pole with a bush on the end, sticking out over a door, means ale for sale. You can drink, gamble, gossip, and catch up on the news. Nobody has a newspaper. The alehouse is your newsfeed.

But keep an eye on the light. Because when the last bell rings, the alehouse has to close.

## The last bell

Evening. Supper is simple. Bread, pottage, whatever's left from dinner. And then you hear it. The curfew bell. The city gates are shut. The taverns close, and the streets are supposed to empty. London's rules said nobody should walk the streets after curfew, unless they were respectable, and carrying a light.

Anyone else was a nightwalker. The watch could arrest you and lock you up until morning. London even built a special lock-up for them, on Cornhill. It was called the Tun. So, the question from this morning. What happens if you're still out when the last bell rings? You're not just late. In this city, being out after dark makes you a suspect. So you go home.

You lie back down on the straw, next to the same three strangers. And it's barely evening. Your whole day ran on daylight. You woke with it, worked by it, ate by it, and now you're going to bed with it. We think of our time as our own. Here, it belonged to the church bells, the sun, and the city council. On the bright side, nobody here can message you after dark. Tomorrow, the bells will wake you again. And you can't hit snooze on a church.
