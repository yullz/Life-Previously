## Your salary weighs something

Your new employer offers a place to live, grain to feed your household, and work on the pharaoh's tomb. You're interested. Then you realise that part of your pay is something you could spill all over the floor. Welcome to Deir el-Medina, in Egypt, about twelve forty BC, during the reign of Ramesses the Second. We're borrowing the lives of the royal tomb workers to test an offer. This is our imaginary recruitment day. The benefits sound promising. The job itself involves making somebody else's burial place. You would like to discuss the benefits again. Would you take grain instead of a cash wage? What happens when you're ill? And can you trust payday? Keep that last question in mind. Later, another generation of workers gives us a very uncomfortable answer. Their records survive across different reigns. We'll keep those dates separate, rather than squeeze every story into this one morning. And these are a particular group of specialists. Their experience cannot stand in for every worker in ancient Egypt.

## A village built around the job

The village sits in the dry hills west of Thebes, near the royal burial grounds. These workers build and decorate tombs. Your imagined new neighbours include people who carve stone, paint walls, and work wood. The employer needs more than strong backs. Inside the settlement, houses stand close together along a central road. A neighbour is considerably easier to find than privacy. Excavated homes commonly have a main living room, smaller spaces behind it, and a kitchen area toward the back. A bench can run along a wall. A column supports the roof. Stairs provide access to the space above. The houses change over time. Our reconstruction shows a typical arrangement, rather than pretending we know the address you'd receive. Still, housing is part of the support described for this community. That gives the offer more substance than a flattering job title. You have somewhere to bring your pay. Which creates your next problem: how much of the house will your salary occupy? Set the grain down. Before you judge its value, you need to understand what receiving provisions means for a household.

## How do you spend grain?

The workmen receive grain as part of their remuneration, alongside other supplies. Pay supports living here, rather than arriving as coins. Don't mistake that for a free meal handed over after every shift. The documented arrangements include recurring provision deliveries. Before those provisions become dinner, someone has work to do. The grain arrives without anybody offering to do the washing up. Service workers help sustain the community too, including people carrying water and grinding grain. Their labour belongs in the picture. So the apparent convenience of your offer rests partly on work being done by other people. Keep them in your calculation. Now step outside our opening year and look at another village record. It describes a bull being bought with goods. Among them are linen and oil. The record gives values in copper units, although the payment it lists consists of goods. A price expressed in metal does not, by itself, mean a handful of minted coins changed hands in that transaction. You can put away the imaginary cash machine. The useful question is what your provisions let your household obtain and use. That record doesn't give us a clean conversion into your salary today. Any neat number would hide more than it explains. Back to the job offer. Housing and provisions sound useful. We have been conveniently quiet about the work required in return.

## The work behind the benefits

From the village, a route crosses the western hills toward the Valley of the Kings. Your workplace is beyond the houses. Picture making that journey with a day's work ahead. The scenery is impressive; it has not volunteered to carry anything. At the royal tomb, different skills contribute to the same project. Cutting and decorating a burial place takes coordinated work. The community's records describe a crew divided into two sides, with supervisors and scribes keeping the operation organised. That doesn't establish a modern shift rota. Nor can we hand you a reliable eight-hour promise from these sources. Think about the task in front of you: a surface to prepare, an area to finish, somebody depending on your contribution. Another surviving community record concerns linen supplied for lamp wicks. Even lighting materials enter the administrative record. It's a small detail, but a useful one. The spectacular finished monument also requires ordinary supplies and people keeping accounts. The scribal equivalent of checking the stationery cupboard has survived considerably longer than anyone involved could have expected. There is room for craft and pride here too. The workers use their skills in funerary monuments for their own families. That tells us they create things beyond the royal commission. It doesn't tell us everyone loves the job, every day. And on a day when you don't appear at the royal tomb, there may be another record waiting for your name.

## Your absence is recorded

Return to Ramesses the Second's fortieth year, our opening frame. An absence register from this period survives as EA five six three four. It records men missing from the royal work. Illness appears in the surviving absence evidence, alongside other reasons for being away. So an empty place at work can become an entry in a record. Your absence has acquired a longer lifespan than you. But this is where the tempting claim about an enormous ancient holiday allowance starts to fall apart. An absence from the royal tomb need not mean a day spent relaxing. A man could be working somewhere else. Crossing his name off your imagined workday count would not tell you how he actually spent those hours. A missing worker, an approved break, and leisure are different things. An attendance record cannot automatically turn one into another. You wouldn't want somebody reconstructing your entire life from the days you failed to turn up at one particular building. One question still matters to your decision: if the reason you are absent is illness, does your support disappear?

## Sick pay, with limits

For that, we need the wider body of village evidence, across several reigns. We're no longer describing one particular morning. Egyptologist Anne Austin concludes that the state provided this workforce with paid sick leave and a doctor. For a worker worried about feeding a household while ill, support like that could make the job worth keeping. But the same research also identifies pressure to maintain progress on the tomb. Support and expectations can sit together. Don't turn that into a modern hospital, unlimited leave, or a rule covering everyone in Egypt. The evidence doesn't support those promises. And having access to a doctor tells us that care is available. It doesn't give us a guarantee of recovery. If you became ill here, you would want more than a promising phrase. Who helps you, and what happens next? Sick pay belongs on the attractive side of your offer. Just leave some space beside it for the questions still unanswered. Let's suppose the arrangement sounds acceptable to you. There is still the rest of life, waiting after the royal work.

## Life beyond the royal tomb

A house full of provisions is not a house where all the work is finished. Someone still has to prepare the food. The community includes families and support workers, whose contribution is easy to lose when we focus on the men making royal tombs. Your own day might be going well. A household's working day is a much larger thing than the hours on your record. Some royal workers also produce funerary equipment at home as a sideline. Time away from one workplace can include another job. Here is another reason to be careful with that imaginary holiday total. An unfinished piece at home will not finish itself. A family tomb shows what someone made or commissioned. Whether its owner enjoyed starting work is a rather different question. If you're weighing the offer, count the whole arrangement: the work you perform, the support you receive, and what remains to do. You may still decide it looks surprisingly appealing. Housing, supplies, skilled work, support when ill: there is something substantial to consider. Until now, though, we have assumed the provisions arrive. To test that assumption, we must leave our opening generation behind.

## When payday fails

Now move generations forward: Ramesses the Third, year twenty-nine, in the twelfth century BC. Different workers, a later crisis. The Turin Strike Papyrus records a dispute over delayed provisions. The scribe Amunnakht writes down what happens. The museum's translation has the workers calling out, 'We are hungry.' That makes the missing delivery considerably less abstract. The workers stop work and press their demands at temple sites. They want the supplies that have not reached them. This is where the grain at the beginning changes meaning. A delayed provision delivery is also a problem for feeding people. The record does not give us a neat ending in which one brave speech solves everything. Deliveries and renewed protests follow. Getting some grain through is not the same as making the whole arrangement reliable. The workers have to keep pressing. These later events don't prove every earlier payday failed. We mustn't quietly move this crisis back into Ramesses the Second's reign. They do show a weakness in the bargain: even a royal connection cannot put provisions in your hands by itself. And they show workers acting, not simply waiting. Their recorded demands deserve to be part of the job story too. Now we can return to the offer we started with. Would you accept it, knowing both the support and the dependence?

## Would you take the job?

Compared with a job offering no dependable support when you are ill, that aspect of the village arrangement could look attractive. That is a comparison with a particular kind of job. It isn't proof that ancient workers, as a group, had better lives. Housing and provisions belong on the positive side. So does the chance to practise a valued craft, if that suits you. The physical work, the limits of what the records tell us, and dependence on deliveries belong in the same decision. For our imaginary applicant, I would admire the support and hesitate over the dependence. Your priorities could produce a different answer. The useful discovery is that these people negotiated a recognisable problem: making the rewards of work cover the needs of life. They aren't merely the anonymous background to a pharaoh's monument. Their skills, households, absences and demands leave their own evidence. So, would you take the offer? Tell us which matters most in your answer: the support, the work, or trusting payday. Meanwhile, your imaginary salary is still sitting by the door. At least you know precisely what you would be carrying home. For another life to step into, visit Pompeii with us next. Its walls have rather a lot to say.
