# Could You Get Through Medieval London Without a Fine?

## A quiet walk, apparently

You are walking to your lodgings. No stolen purse, no fight. Then the watch stops you. Your problem is the hour. Or your explanation. Or the awkward fact that nobody here seems to know who you are. Could you cross medieval London without a fine? And what does this city want from a stranger like you? Hold that second question. We begin earlier, around the year thirteen sixty-four. You have somewhere to sleep and would prefer not to replace it with Newgate. Our rules survive in the Liber Albus, a collection of London's laws and customs. They tell us what authorities prescribed. Later, a court entry supplies an actual outcome. For now, your challenge has three temptations: carry something impressive, win an argument, and stay out a little longer. Your ambition is modest: reach bedtime with your money and freedom intact. That should be easy. You can hear the confidence that makes this an excellent afternoon for somebody else. At your lodging, a sword lies on a bench. Our imagined prop makes a poor suggestion. Carry that, and perhaps nobody will bother you. The innkeeper has a different view, with a reason beyond disliking scratches on the furniture. Your first decision arrives before the street. Does looking ready for trouble help you avoid it?

## Leave the sword behind

The ordinance prohibits going armed in city or suburbs, day or night. An ordinary visitor has no sword exemption. The rule has exceptions, but they describe particular duties. Looking expensive is not one of the duties listed. A lord's attendant may carry his master's sword while accompanying him. That relationship matters. Other exceptions include royal serjeants-at-arms, city officers, and people accompanying officers at their command to help preserve the peace. So seeing somebody armed does not tell you that you may copy him. You may be copying the exception, without qualifying for it. Break the rule and the ordinance adds confiscation: besides the penalty, you lose the arms you were carrying. And your host has obligations too. Guests are supposed to be warned to leave their arms where they are lodging. If a guest carries weapons because the host failed to warn them, the host can face imprisonment and a fine. Your innkeeper has a practical interest in your behaviour. You are not the only person who might regret this accessory. You leave the sword. Your host closes the cupboard with the air of a man who has had this conversation before. No heroic flourish, no portrait commissioned. Leaving a weapon on a bench is the least cinematic victory in medieval London. One temptation avoided. You step into London with empty hands, which will shortly become relevant to the next bad idea.

## The price of winning an argument

Two people are arguing in our invented courtyard. You are certain you could explain who is being unreasonable. Unhelpful confidence again. The ordinance distinguishes drawing a weapon, using it, and striking with a fist. Under this particular London ordinance, drawing a sword or knife can be punishable even if you never land a blow. The prescribed alternative is half a mark, or fifteen days in Newgate. That is six shillings and eight pence, or imprisonment. The word 'or' matters. We are not adding both punishments together; the ordinance offers one or the other. If the weapon draws blood, that rises to twenty shillings or forty days. No injury illustration required. Keeping the knife sheathed is useful. It does not make punching your neighbour into a clever, inexpensive alternative. A fist blow without blood carries three shillings or eight days. Drawing blood with the fist raises that to forty pence or twelve days. Different categories in this ordinance, rather than a universal price list for every medieval town. More awkwardly, paying the city does not erase the injured person's separate claim. The text preserves their ability to seek legal recovery. The city's fine is not a fee for being allowed to hit somebody you dislike. Before release, these offenders must also find people to guarantee good behaviour. Money is not the only thing being asked for. Tonight, you will face the same awkward question: who will answer for you? The best outcome is uneventful. You do not join the quarrel, however well you could have settled it. Nobody gets punched, no blade leaves its sheath, and your money remains available for things that are actually pleasant. You avoided a fight. Surely a peaceful walk is simpler. London attaches conditions to that too.

## The bell changes the walk

Light is fading. You are causing no disturbance, and your lodging is an imagined short walk away. Then the curfew sounds. For this ordinance, the named church is Our Lady at Bow, now called St Mary-le-Bow. The bell is to ring between day and night. Our source does not turn that into a universal nine o'clock. Changing light matters. The rule does not wait for your watch to display a convenient number. It forbids wandering after curfew, unless you fit an exception. That last part is where a simple explanation often goes wrong. This is not a claim that nobody may ever be outside after dark. We need to read the conditions. But ignoring the rule can have more serious consequences than an embarrassed conversation about why you lost track of time. The ordinance directs that somebody found wandering contrary to it be taken to Newgate. A casual stroll has acquired a destination. Release requires a fine and good surety for future behaviour. This clause does not give us a fixed fine to display. In other words, the problem can involve both payment and somebody prepared to stand behind your promise to behave. For a newcomer, your excellent opinion of yourself is not somebody else's guarantee. You have a better idea: get a lantern. Useful—but it is only one part of the exception.

## The lantern is not a permit

The selected ordinance allows a known man of good reputation, or his servant, out for good cause and with a light. The light does not replace the other conditions. You cannot buy your whole explanation as a candle. Nor does simply claiming you have an errand settle the question. The wording also cares who you are connected to. Imagine a known household's servant on a legitimate errand, and an unexplained stranger, walking the same lane. They may look equally harmless to you. The ordinance gives the first a relationship that the second cannot simply borrow. That does not tell us the result of every encounter. It tells us why reputation and purpose belong in the picture. You have no local reputation. That friendly smile is doing a lot of work, without references. The practical lesson for this fictional challenge is to return earlier. You cannot count on a lantern to answer every question. Going back before curfew avoids this particular nightwalking problem. It does not create a magical exemption from every other city rule. Notice how different this feels from waving a modern card at a scanner. The important connections are between people. This exception belongs to its society. We cannot rewrite it as a universal right of night travel. For you, arriving indoors early means losing a little evening freedom in order to keep control of where the night ends.

## Who is watching the street?

Meanwhile, who is supposed to notice the trouble? The ordinance places responsibility on the city's wards and their aldermen. Each alderman is to arrange proper watches. If neglect allows trouble, the ward and its alderman are made answerable. This gives the neighbourhood a stake in keeping the peace. It is not a picture of a modern police station on every corner. The alderman is also to hold names of inhabitants and lodgers, including people in less visible workplaces. A list required by a rule is not proof that every list was complete. But you can see the ambition. Authorities wanted to know who belonged in the ward and who was staying with whom. Your lodging connects you to that system. There is also an arrest provision for reputable men of standing when officers are absent. They can bring offenders to the sheriffs. That is broader than waiting for one professional uniform. It is not permission for anybody to attack a stranger they dislike. The repeated concern is peace, but the means include neighbours, hosts, civic officers and people willing to answer for others. Being unknown makes our challenge difficult. Connections could change how an encounter unfolds. Yet a rulebook cannot show us exactly how an evening ended. For that, we need a record of an actual case. Move forward to March thirteen sixty-five. Three named men appear in the city's records, and the ending deserves a closer look.

## A real case complicates the rules

The men are William Moot, John de Henham and Thomas Fraunceys. We know their names from the calendar of court records. They faced several charges together: a night-time affray, keeping doors open after curfew, and nightwalking against the peace. This was not a record of three people punished merely for taking a peaceful stroll. The combination matters. The entry says they were committed to prison. Then, on the same day, they were released on mainprise to keep the peace. That means their release came with guarantees about keeping the peace. It does not mean the record has declared the entire affair imaginary. The entry gives no fine amount. An attractive price tag would invent information. Nor can we use this one outcome to promise every arrested traveller a same-day exit. It is one recorded case. What it gives us is a useful contrast: the ordinance sets out expectations, while a court record preserves a particular response. Their names change the scale. People had an evening go wrong; they are not anonymous medieval scenery. The entry also gives trades and a ward: a brewer and two hackneymen, horse-hirers, in Candlewick Street Ward. We cannot recover their conversation. We can keep what it records: charges, prison, and release the same day, with guarantees.

## An uneventful victory

Back to your own evening, in thirteen sixty-four. You would rather learn from the documents than become an entry in them. Your best route through the challenge has been remarkably unheroic: leave the sword, leave the argument, and be indoors before the bell. If you must travel later, the exception asks for more than a light: a known name, or a known master, and a reason. If trouble does happen, the rules distinguish different acts, and they ask the same thing afterwards: who will stand surety for you? The real case ends more quietly than a headline: three men committed, then released the same day, with people answering for them. Now the held question: what does this city want from a stranger? Not your money first. It wants to know who you are. Remember the watchman from the start? With a host who knows you and a lantern, that conversation ends differently. Probably. Which would have caught you first: carrying the sword, stepping into the argument, or assuming your lantern settled the matter?
