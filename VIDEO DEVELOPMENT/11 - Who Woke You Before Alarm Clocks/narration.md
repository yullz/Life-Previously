# Who Woke You Up Before You Had an Alarm Clock?

## A city wakes while one worker goes to bed

Manchester, eighteen sixty-two. The night has been wet, and a man with a long wand is finally going home to bed. His job is to get other people out of bed, so they can begin their work. Imagine the service arriving at your bedroom window: tap, tap. Someone outside expects more from you than a sleepy groan. That person is a knocker-up. Paying someone to wake you raises two questions. Why pay a human being when clocks already exist? And, more urgently, who makes sure the human being wakes up? A Victorian account answers that second question. It is less mysterious than the usual guessing game. First, forget the idea that everyone was waiting for somebody to invent an alarm clock. Alarms were already old. A London-made clock in the Science Museum Group collection has an alarm and dates from the sixteen hundreds. Owning a clock, hearing it, and leaving bed are different problems. A waking service could help with the last one. Waugh was writing during the cotton famine. This is not a picture of every mill running happily. He notices coffee stalls packing up and the knocker-up heading home. Even before breakfast, different working days are already pulling apart. One woman's detailed recollections help explain what it took to make that morning service a business.

## Mrs Waters finds her first customer

In eighteen seventy-seven, Chambers's Journal published the recollections of Mrs Waters. The writer leaves her northern-English town unnamed, apart from an initial. We cannot turn her into our Manchester worker. What follows is her reported story, illustrated rather than independently verified. She said her husband injured his back at the foundry and could no longer work. The household needed another way to earn. An opportunity came through somebody wanting an early start. He offered to pay her to wake him at three. This was not simply another factory worker rushing for the same bell. She described him as a man doing design work. He said quiet mornings suited him. Unfortunately, good intentions the evening before did not reliably produce an awake designer. According to Waters, he could sleep through an alarm, or wake and drift off again before it finished making its noise. The purchase was therefore quite specific: another person would come to the house and make getting up harder to postpone. She accepted. And she said that within a year she had thirty customers, most needing her between five and six. One unusually early customer had opened a door into a wider market. Other people also needed help keeping their morning promises. The useful part was a person prepared to be outside while the customer stayed warm inside. But making the loudest possible noise is surprisingly bad business when you wake a whole round of customers.

## Why the window mattered

Waters described an earlier method: knocking or ringing at the front door. It could wake the person who had paid you. It could also wake the neighbours, who had paid nothing. Some did not want your service at any price that morning. Neighbours were disturbed, while the knocker-up was accidentally providing several households with a free wake-up call. A fishing-rod-like wand offered a targeted approach: tap the bedroom window close to the sleeper you actually mean. The trick was contact at the right window. This was tapping glass, not smashing it, and certainly not poking through it. Then came the useful bit: a reply. Waters remembered customers answering with a tap, footsteps, or a few words from the window. A clock can finish while you fall asleep. A person outside can notice whether anybody has answered. Sending a signal is easier than knowing somebody has acted on it. The reply makes a difference. The relationship could be pleasant. Waters said a friendly greeting made a difference after several bad-tempered customers had already answered her. It could also wear thin. She remembered giving up one regular payer because he was repeatedly insulting when she woke him. Payment apparently did not buy an unlimited right to abuse the person delivering the service. She could stop coming. But once dozens of people depend on you, how do you get around before everybody is late?

## The route and the price

Waters said her round grew to around eighty houses, sometimes more. Those figures are her recollection, not a surviving customer database. Her explanation was organization: learn shortcuts, keep moving, and get good at rousing people without lingering beneath one window. Two distant customers cannot both receive your personal attention at exactly the same instant. So a list of addresses is not enough. Timing and distance matter, and they appeared in the prices she described. Her very first customer offered half a crown a week. Once other early customers joined, she said she reduced his charge. The point was not one fixed price for everybody. An awkwardly early call could cost substantially more than a later one nearby. Before four, she reported charging eighteenpence a week. Soon after four, the price was a shilling. These were weekly charges. Between five and six, she described charges ranging from sixpence down to threepence, depending on the time and the distance. Most customers were in that cheaper group. The especially early designer was not the standard customer. A small recurring payment could reduce the risk of a much worse morning. Waters described clients worrying about losing money for lateness. A missed start could make the wake-up service look like money well spent. But a price list does not collect itself. Once everybody is awake, you still have to get paid.

## The second half of the business

She said most payments came on Saturdays. Some customers called at her house; others left money with people she appointed. For slow payers, her warning was direct: fall behind, and you might find yourself allowed to oversleep one morning. That was leverage only because the customers valued the service. It depended on their needing her again next week. She also claimed exceptions for people struggling with sickness or large families. Her account includes judgement, not just a tariff. And the morning round was not her only business. After she began knocking up, Waters opened a shop. She sold practical household goods, including brushes, blacking, pots and pans. They did not need selling before they spoiled. She explained that choice in business terms: little perishable stock, less worry about changing fashions, and customers bringing money. Her shop and her waking round could fit into different parts of the day. That does not mean the day was easy. She said she did not sleep during the day because she had a shop to mind. Hold that thought. How much night remained once she had finished everything else? We will come back to that. For now, there is another mistake to avoid. Customers who struggled to wake were not necessarily people with nothing to do. Waters remembered two sisters whose evenings help explain why somebody might need a knock after a night's sleep.

## What happened before bedtime

The sisters worked at a mill, she said. They had saved enough to take and furnish a room together. That room also needed cleaning, and their clothes needed mending. Some of the work continued after the paid working day. They went to bed tired and sometimes late. A missed morning could follow an evening spent keeping ordinary life together. It is easy to make the human alarm clock into a joke. The workload behind the service is less funny. Waters recalled these customers warmly. They greeted her kindly, insisted on paying, and sometimes invited her for Sunday tea. That was a different relationship from the angry customer at the window. The same service could produce very different mornings. It also complicates the idea that this was just a person behaving like a machine. Customers knew who was coming. The job included remembering people, collecting payments, finding routes, and putting up with the weather. The tapping was the visible part. Even her account of success needs care. It is a story told by a retired worker through an anonymous writer. The article's closing note warns that competition had grown. Her reported results were not a promise for anybody picking up a stick. Nor should one woman's schedule become a rule for the whole trade. Our Manchester observer had already shown a different pattern. The Manchester knocker-up was heading to bed. Waters had a shop to open after her round.

## Who woke the person waking everyone else?

Who woke her? The account answers without leaving us with an endless chain of knockers-up. Waters said she went to bed at nine, except on Saturdays, and got up again at half past two. Her earliest customer lived about twenty minutes away and wanted waking at three. There was not much spare morning to lose. But when she was younger, she said, she slept heavily after her active days. Habit alone was not the original solution. Her husband woke her. And she explicitly credits an alarm as well. The human alarm service could depend on an alarm. That detail matters. These were overlapping ways to manage time, not a neat world of humans suddenly replaced by machines. She said that after several years she began waking naturally, which became important when her husband was no longer alive. That is her experience, not advice to train yourself out of needing sleep, and not an explanation for every knocker-up. She also remembered making tea for her husband after the earliest call. Paid work and care occupied the same small hours. Read the account closely and the clever waking trick turns into a household working out how to support itself. There is a business outside the window. There is another set of responsibilities waiting for its owner back inside. None of this made a cold, wet street feel like an extra hour under the blanket.

## A small job holding up a morning

Rain and wind were among the difficulties Waters remembered most sharply. Slushy streets made a working round less charming than its postcard version. When she retired, she said, she passed the knocking-up work to a neighbour who needed it. A route was more than a stick. It meant customers who expected someone, at a particular window, at a particular time. That is why the service makes more sense once we stop treating it as proof that Victorian people had no clocks. Some needed help waking. Some needed an answer demanded from the street. The account describes a market built around those ordinary failures. Its providers made their own calculations about time, distance, payment, family, and the next working day. Back in Waugh's Manchester, the coffee stalls are closing and the man with the long wand is going home. We cannot give every knocker-up his schedule, or give every customer Mrs Waters' prices. These are particular windows into the work. But together they show something easily missed: one person's start to the day could be the result of somebody else's labour. The customer might remember a few taps on the glass. Behind those taps were a route, a payment, and another tired person. So the next time an alarm rings, consider the alternative: someone outside, already dressed, waiting for you to prove you are awake. The noise was the smallest part of the service. The expensive part was having another human being care whether you got up.
