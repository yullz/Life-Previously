# When Beaver Fur Was Money: What Could You Buy?

## Your money has fur

Twenty fishhooks. That is one option. A blanket is another—but the blanket will swallow six times as much of your budget. You have arrived with fur, and you would like to leave with things your household can actually use. Before you agree to anything, there are three questions. What counts as money, what can it buy, and who gets to decide? Welcome to Fort Albany on James Bay, in seventeen thirty-three. We are following an imagined visit using a real trade list. The list measures value in beaver skins. The accounting term you will hear is made beaver: the value of a prime pelt. That matters. A bundle of ten skins does not automatically mean ten identical units, regardless of the skins inside it. You can also bring other furs. They are valued against the beaver standard, which gives different goods a common measuring stick. Think of the unit as the language of the deal. The actual things changing hands still have to be examined. And the blanket hanging behind the counter is already making our little shopping problem harder. Six units for one item. Why does the fur have that much purchasing power? The answer takes us a long way from this trading room. Somebody on the other side of the Atlantic wants a hat. Your household wants useful goods. This trade connects those wishes. But getting from a beaver to a shopping budget involves rather more work than walking up to a counter.

## The work inside the bundle

For Cree traders in this region, the journey to bayside posts could be made in birchbark canoes. The fur trade fitted around seasonal work. Families also needed food, shelter and the resources to keep going through the year. Winter was an important season for acquiring the pelts that traders wanted. We are briefly stepping back from the summer landing. Now our tidy unit of value contains weather, travel and labour. The price list does not tell us how many hours each pelt required. Across the ocean, the sought-after material is the beaver's soft underfur. That is the part especially useful for making felt. We should picture a hat-maker working with felt, rather than someone attaching a complete furry skin to a hat. The finished object can look very different from what left James Bay. The material has changed form along the way. That distant demand helps explain why a bundle on this counter can be exchanged for cloth, metal goods or sugar. It does not mean everyone in the chain wants the same thing. A trader bringing fur is also a customer choosing imported goods. The surviving list comes to us in Arthur Dobbs's book, published eleven years after the year it describes. It lets us test specific prices. It does not preserve our imaginary visitor's receipt, or every conversation that could change a deal. So let us begin with something small enough to overlook. On this list, one unit can buy twenty fishhooks.

## Small goods, real choices

Twenty fishhooks for one beaver unit. Put them beside eight knives, which the same list also values at one unit. Those are alternatives. Handing over one unit does not buy both piles, however enthusiastically our Visitor points at them. A small object can have a very practical job. These are choices about equipment, rather than a cupboard full of curiosities. Now try clothing. One pair of breeches costs three units in the printed schedule. Knee-length trousers take a larger bite. The blanket costs six. You cannot infer the price just from whether an object looks large or small on the shelf. The arithmetic is straightforward. The decision is personal: warmth, clothing, tools, or something else your household needs. Our list also offers sugar: two pounds for one unit. A shopping decision can include wants as well as essential equipment. There is no universal basket everyone must take home. A standard unit makes the alternatives comparable; it does not choose them for you. Even the word 'one' needs attention. One pair, one pound, one yard: those are very different quantities. A yard of broadcloth is listed at two units. A pound of thread is also two. The unit beside the good matters. If we flatten that table into 'one object for one beaver', we change the deal before anyone has even agreed to it. And that mistake becomes especially obvious when we pick up the kettle.

## The kettle trap

The important word here is pound. The list prices brass kettles at one pound of kettle for one beaver unit. That is not a promise that every kettle on the shelf costs one. A heavier one changes the arithmetic. For a simple example, a three-pound kettle would work out at three units under that rate. We are calculating, not reading a receipt. You would also care about the object itself. Its listed weight cannot tell you whether it is the kettle you actually want. The same caution applies elsewhere. A number helps compare a trade; it does not replace looking closely at the goods. The schedule even gives guns a range of ten, eleven or twelve units. There is no instruction here to stack pelts to barrel height. Powder, shot and flints have their own entries. The equipment in a trade is not captured by one dramatic picture of a gun. Put the price list down for a moment. The person on the other side of the counter has standards too. Museum research describes customers checking the quality of imported cloth and demanding better stock when it was defective. That is a very different scene from someone accepting anything shiny. The choice concerns what the goods are like in use. A common measuring unit can make a discussion easier. It cannot force a dissatisfied customer to like the fabric. And cloth leaves a surprisingly small clue behind in the archaeological record.

## The customer inspects the goods

Cloth itself can decay. The small lead seals attached to bales are much more visible to archaeologists studying the trade. A seal could record a standard of length, weave or colour, or show that a tax had been paid. It was not a little beaver coin. A small metal disc in a trading story can have an entirely different job. English seals have been found at Fort Albany. The museum also compares seals from French trading posts farther south. Those finds help reveal an appetite for imported textiles, including different weights and colours—not simply whatever cloth happened to arrive. The museum describes traders looking for suppliers who could satisfy that demand before competitors did. So the shopper with fur is helping shape what crosses the ocean in the other direction. We can see both sides now. European buyers value beaver fur. Indigenous consumers assess the goods offered in return. That does not tell us every bargain was fair. It does tell us to take the customers' judgment seriously. Nor should we assume the price stays the same when we move to a different post. The note above this very list warns us about that. It compares Albany and Moose with York and Churchill. The difference, in Dobbs's explanation, is competition.

## A price is not a law of nature

Dobbs says the standard at York and Churchill is higher, because French traders are not as close to challenge the Company there. Around Albany and Moose, he says French competition interferes more with the Company's trade. His price list arrives with an argument attached. That helps explain why we have stayed with one place and year. Another post's numbers would make a different shopping basket. It does not mean every customer can casually walk down the road to a rival shop. Distance and relationships still matter. The post sits inside a much wider world of journeys, households and trading parties. Our counter scene shows only one part. In an earlier account from Albany, seventeen oh-six, a leader named Whatting arrived with twenty canoes. He received a coat, tobacco, knives and a hatchet. This was a gift to a trading-party leader, not our universal shopping allowance. The example shows why a list of prices cannot capture every exchange that mattered around the post. Leadership and personal relationships belonged in the story alongside bundles and arithmetic. We now return to our seventeen thirty-three example. There is another complication. Sometimes goods could be advanced before the furs that would repay them arrived. That changes the question from 'what can I take home today?' to 'what will this relationship require later?' Fur as money does not make the future disappear.

## The goods before the furs

Historian Richard Preston places the beginning of equipment credit at Moose and Albany sometime between seventeen twenty-six and seventeen thirty-seven. Supplies could be given on the expectation of later fur returns. The arrangement connected more than a single visit. We should not turn that into a modern payment plan, complete with invented instalments. The evidence describes its own relationships and obligations. Nor does it mean every person here has the same debt. Our price-list exercise cannot supply somebody's missing account. It does mean a shopper's position could depend on what had already been promised, as well as what was on the counter. And a household could value the same item differently according to what it already owned or needed. Our imagined six-unit budget is deliberately simpler. We are using it to understand the list, not impersonate an actual family's accounts. We have checked quantities, noticed quality and kept the prices in their setting. Now we can make a choice without changing the rules midway. The first option is easy to describe: one blanket. The printed price uses all six units. The other option starts with the breeches at three. That leaves three units to spend on smaller purchases. We can buy the twenty hooks, the eight knives and the two pounds of sugar—one unit for each listed quantity. Add them to the breeches, and the total is six. Two very different ways to use the same budget.

## What would you take home?

Here is the choice: one blanket, or a pair of breeches, twenty hooks, eight knives and two pounds of sugar. Both use six units in this exercise. That does not tell us which is the better choice for a real household. If warmth is the urgent problem, a collection of small objects may be a poor substitute for the blanket you came for. If you already have bedding, the mixed basket may look more useful. The price list gives comparisons, not a shopping conscience. And before saying yes, you still need to look at the goods, agree on the value of the furs and understand the terms. That is what our strange-looking currency has revealed. This is a conversation about quality, needs and bargaining, as well as numbers. The beaver unit makes different things comparable. It does not make every pelt identical, every post equal, or every transaction fair. So: which basket would you take home? And what would you want to inspect before you handed your fur over? A list that starts with beaver skins has led us to household decisions we can recognise, even when the money looks unfamiliar. Behind the bundle are people's work and journeys. Behind the imported goods are other people's demands, plans and expectations. The counter is where those stories meet. What leaves it matters just as much as what arrives. And our Visitor has learned to ask one more question before choosing a kettle: how much does it weigh?
