TITLE: When Beaver Fur Was Money: What Could You Buy?

## Your money has fur
::: S001 | One simple iron fishhook on timber; room for local quantity and price card. [pose: eyebrow-raise 0.8,0.94 0.6] [note: Dobbs, 1744 · 1733 trade list, pp.193–194] [zoom-in]
Twenty fishhooks. That is one option. A blanket is another—but the blanket will swallow six times as much of your budget.

::: S002 | Two adult trading visitors unload a closed dry fur bundle from a birchbark canoe; no animal carcass. [pan-left]
You have arrived with fur, and you would like to leave with things your household can actually use.

::: S003 | Three-question opening card, one question revealed per cut. [still]
Before you agree to anything, there are three questions. What counts as money, what can it buy, and who gets to decide?

::: S004 | Reframe river landing with low timber store partly behind trees; Visitor on unobstructed near bank. [pose: look-around 0.8,0.94 0.6] [note: FORT ALBANY · JAMES BAY · 1733] [same-as: S002] [pan-right]
Welcome to Fort Albany on James Bay, in seventeen thirty-three. We are following an imagined visit using a real trade list.

::: S005 | One abstract unit square beside a folded fur illustration; clearly explanatory. [note: Archives of Manitoba · Made beaver] [still]
The list measures value in beaver skins. The accounting term you will hear is made beaver: the value of a prime pelt.

::: S006 | Establish room: left window, right shelves and kettle, counter with closed bundles; customers and clerk facing each other. [pan-left]
That matters. A bundle of ten skins does not automatically mean ten identical units, regardless of the skins inside it.

::: S007 | Two differently sized closed fur parcels beside clerk's blank ledger; avoid identifying unsupported animal skins. [same-as: S006] [zoom-out]
You can also bring other furs. They are valued against the beaver standard, which gives different goods a common measuring stick.

::: S008 | Customer and clerk both assess folded fur on counter with calm attentive faces. [pose: closeup-thoughtful 0.8,1.02 0.82] [same-as: S006] [pan-right]
Think of the unit as the language of the deal. The actual things changing hands still have to be examined.

::: S009 | Plain unstriped folded wool blanket, distinct from later HBC point blankets. [note: Dobbs · p.193, row15] [same-as: S001] [zoom-in]
And the blanket hanging behind the counter is already making our little shopping problem harder. Six units for one item.

::: S010 | Look past fur bundle toward open doorway and river light; preserve counter landmarks. [same-as: S006] [pan-left]
Why does the fur have that much purchasing power? The answer takes us a long way from this trading room.

::: S011 | Folded fur at left and dark felt hat silhouette at right; no whole animal hat. [pose: count-fingers 0.8,0.94 0.6] [same-as: S001] [zoom-out]
Somebody on the other side of the Atlantic wants a hat. Your household wants useful goods. This trade connects those wishes.

::: S012 | Canoe cargo at low landing, adults lift a compact bundle carefully; no extravagant treasure chest. [same-as: S002] [pan-right]
But getting from a beaver to a shopping budget involves rather more work than walking up to a counter.

## The work inside the bundle
::: S013 | Close birchbark canoe side with ribs and secured cargo; one wooden paddle; no later cargo boat. [note: Carlos & Lewis, 2010 · regional context] [same-as: S002] [zoom-in]
For Cree traders in this region, the journey to bayside posts could be made in birchbark canoes.

::: S014 | Adults sort modest travel bundles beside canoe; no invented numbered household ration. [same-as: S002] [pan-left]
The fur trade fitted around seasonal work. Families also needed food, shelter and the resources to keep going through the year.

::: S015 | Winter woodland explanatory anchor with two fully clothed adults walking with closed bundle; no trapping action. [note: EARLIER WINTER · REGIONAL CONTEXT] [zoom-out]
Winter was an important season for acquiring the pelts that traders wanted. We are briefly stepping back from the summer landing.

::: S016 | Snow-dusted footwear and bundle carried above snow; stable adult anatomy. [same-as: S015] [pan-right]
Now our tidy unit of value contains weather, travel and labour. The price list does not tell us how many hours each pelt required.

::: S017 | Simple locally drawn fur cross-section distinguishes long guard hairs and soft underfur; no microscopic realism claim. [note: Canadian Museum of History · Fur trade] [still]
Across the ocean, the sought-after material is the beaver's soft underfur. That is the part especially useful for making felt.

::: S018 | European workshop anchor with dark felt hat bodies, plain wooden blocks, craftsperson at bench. [note: EUROPEAN HAT-MAKING · EXPLANATORY VIEW] [pan-left]
We should picture a hat-maker working with felt, rather than someone attaching a complete furry skin to a hat.

::: S019 | Dark smooth felt hat body on wooden block; no beaver tail or ears. [same-as: S018] [zoom-out]
The finished object can look very different from what left James Bay. The material has changed form along the way.

::: S020 | Fur parcel foreground, folded cloth and kettle in established rear shelf, no lettering. [same-as: S006] [pan-right]
That distant demand helps explain why a bundle on this counter can be exchanged for cloth, metal goods or sugar.

::: S021 | Indigenous customer gestures toward cloth while clerk waits; equal height and agency. [same-as: S006] [zoom-in]
It does not mean everyone in the chain wants the same thing. A trader bringing fur is also a customer choosing imported goods.

::: S022 | Locally typeset date pair with source title shortened; no generated book text. [note: Arthur Dobbs · published1744] [still]
The surviving list comes to us in Arthur Dobbs's book, published eleven years after the year it describes.

::: S023 | Blank ledger page and customer's hand resting beside goods; no fake documentary signature. [pose: scratch-head 0.8,0.94 0.6] [same-as: S006] [zoom-out]
It lets us test specific prices. It does not preserve our imaginary visitor's receipt, or every conversation that could change a deal.

::: S024 | One iron fishhook enlarged for clarity; exact quantity will be shown in next chapter's local graphic. [same-as: S001] [pan-right]
So let us begin with something small enough to overlook. On this list, one unit can buy twenty fishhooks.

## Small goods, real choices
::: S025 | Local two-row price comparison;20hooks in4rows5 and8knives in2rows4, accurate distinct objects. [note: Dobbs · p.194, rows26and40] [still]
Twenty fishhooks for one beaver unit. Put them beside eight knives, which the same list also values at one unit.

::: S026 | Two separated neutral display trays with representative hook and knife; local price labels and OR, not counted generated piles. [pose: count-fingers 0.8,0.94 0.6] [same-as: S001] [pan-left]
Those are alternatives. Handing over one unit does not buy both piles, however enthusiastically our Visitor points at them.

::: S027 | Iron hook and plain utility knife on timber, handle and blade safely at rest. [same-as: S001] [zoom-out]
A small object can have a very practical job. These are choices about equipment, rather than a cupboard full of curiosities.

::: S028 | Plain early-eighteenth-century knee-length breeches laid flat, no modern jeans or full-length trousers. [note: Dobbs · p.194, row22] [same-as: S001] [pan-right]
Now try clothing. One pair of breeches costs three units in the printed schedule. Knee-length trousers take a larger bite.

::: S029 | Folded unstriped blanket beside representative small tool, prices absent until local overlay. [same-as: S001] [zoom-in]
The blanket costs six. You cannot infer the price just from whether an object looks large or small on the shelf.

::: S030 | Customer considers shelves without comic confusion; Visitor remains secondary at frame right. [pose: closeup-thoughtful 0.8,1.02 0.82] [same-as: S006] [pan-left]
The arithmetic is straightforward. The decision is personal: warmth, clothing, tools, or something else your household needs.

::: S031 | Unwrapped pale sugar pieces on plain balance pan; no branded bag or modern measuring label. [note: Dobbs · p.193, row7] [same-as: S001] [zoom-out]
Our list also offers sugar: two pounds for one unit. A shopping decision can include wants as well as essential equipment.

::: S032 | Customer places selected plain cloth beside bundle while leaving other goods on shelves; illustrative decision. [same-as: S006] [pan-right]
There is no universal basket everyone must take home. A standard unit makes the alternatives comparable; it does not choose them for you.

::: S033 | Three clean unit labels with trouser, weight and cloth icons. [still]
Even the word 'one' needs attention. One pair, one pound, one yard: those are very different quantities.

::: S034 | Cloth length and thread bundle in separate zones; quantities and prices added locally. [note: Dobbs · p.193, rows11and14] [same-as: S001] [pan-left]
A yard of broadcloth is listed at two units. A pound of thread is also two. The unit beside the good matters.

::: S035 | Simplified incorrect one-for-one assumption crossed through, correct weight and length labels retained. [still]
If we flatten that table into 'one object for one beaver', we change the deal before anyone has even agreed to it.

::: S036 | Customer lifts simple brass cooking kettle by bail handle just above counter; grounded wrist and correct gravity. [same-as: S006] [pan-right]
And that mistake becomes especially obvious when we pick up the kettle.

## The kettle trap
::: S037 | Exact local price card with kettle icon and weight symbol; not a tea kettle. [note: Dobbs · p.193, row3] [still]
The important word here is pound. The list prices brass kettles at one pound of kettle for one beaver unit.

::: S038 | Two brass cooking kettles with clearly different size, identical construction family; no price painted on them. [same-as: S001] [pan-left]
That is not a promise that every kettle on the shelf costs one. A heavier one changes the arithmetic.

::: S039 | Three unit squares beside locally labelled3lbkettle; label example clearly. [still]
For a simple example, a three-pound kettle would work out at three units under that rate. We are calculating, not reading a receipt.

::: S040 | Hands examine rim and bail fittings of brass cooking vessel; no invented defect assumed. [pose: eyebrow-raise 0.8,0.94 0.6] [same-as: S001] [pan-right]
You would also care about the object itself. Its listed weight cannot tell you whether it is the kettle you actually want.

::: S041 | Customer looks along cloth edge while clerk holds opposite end; no customer passively accepting unwanted item. [same-as: S006] [zoom-in]
The same caution applies elsewhere. A number helps compare a trade; it does not replace looking closely at the goods.

::: S042 | Local text-only historical rate card with small neutral long-gun silhouette, no handling demonstration. [note: Dobbs · p.194, row30] [still]
The schedule even gives guns a range of ten, eleven or twelve units. There is no instruction here to stack pelts to barrel height.

::: S043 | Closed plain powder container, lead-shot pouch and flint shown separately at rest; no loading or ignition. [same-as: S001] [zoom-out]
Powder, shot and flints have their own entries. The equipment in a trade is not captured by one dramatic picture of a gun.

::: S044 | Indigenous customer's focused expression, cloth in hands; clerk attentive rather than dominant. [same-as: S006] [pan-right]
Put the price list down for a moment. The person on the other side of the counter has standards too.

::: S045 | Establish macro cloth view tied to counter geometry; customer examines weave near window. [note: Canadian Museum of History · Sealed and Delivered] [zoom-in]
Museum research describes customers checking the quality of imported cloth and demanding better stock when it was defective.

::: S046 | Fingers test cloth edge and drape, restrained texture rather than sparkles or treasure. [same-as: S045] [pan-left]
That is a very different scene from someone accepting anything shiny. The choice concerns what the goods are like in use.

::: S047 | Customer sets one cloth bolt aside and looks to another; reconstruction, no staged angry confrontation. [pose: watch-arms-crossed 0.8,0.94 0.6] [same-as: S045] [zoom-out]
A common measuring unit can make a discussion easier. It cannot force a dissatisfied customer to like the fabric.

::: S048 | Tiny dull lead seal beside folded cloth on neutral surface, diagrammatic form without invented readable mark. [same-as: S001] [pan-right]
And cloth leaves a surprisingly small clue behind in the archaeological record.

## The customer inspects the goods
::: S049 | Schematic cloth-bale seal enlarged beside soft fabric silhouette, labelled as explanatory. [note: Canadian Museum of History · bale seals] [same-as: S001] [zoom-in]
Cloth itself can decay. The small lead seals attached to bales are much more visible to archaeologists studying the trade.

::: S050 | Three cloth-standard icons and separate tax-record icon; no promise that every seal certifies all four. [still]
A seal could record a standard of length, weave or colour, or show that a tax had been paid.

::: S051 | Lead seal attached by short strip to cloth wrapping, not loose spending token; no currency symbol. [pose: eyebrow-raise 0.8,0.94 0.6] [same-as: S001] [zoom-out]
It was not a little beaver coin. A small metal disc in a trading story can have an entirely different job.

::: S052 | Simple locally drawn site list; no unverified exact map route or French seal attributed to Albany. [note: Fort Albany / Fort Frontenac / Fort Michilimackinac] [still]
English seals have been found at Fort Albany. The museum also compares seals from French trading posts farther south.

::: S053 | Several restrained cloth rolls in brown, muted red and blue; customers compare two, not a modern fabric shop. [same-as: S045] [zoom-in]
Those finds help reveal an appetite for imported textiles, including different weights and colours—not simply whatever cloth happened to arrive.

::: S054 | Clerk compares stock with customer's chosen sample; blank ledger on counter, no invented written order. [same-as: S045] [pan-left]
The museum describes traders looking for suppliers who could satisfy that demand before competitors did.

::: S055 | Secured cloth bundle loaded into same canoe at landing; fur parcel remains back at post, illustrative exchange. [same-as: S002] [zoom-out]
So the shopper with fur is helping shape what crosses the ocean in the other direction.

::: S056 | Two-way arrow between labelled fur and imported goods, equal visual weight; no universal win-win badge. [still]
We can see both sides now. European buyers value beaver fur. Indigenous consumers assess the goods offered in return.

::: S057 | Customer pauses over proposed goods, clerk waits; avoid smiles implying universal satisfaction. [pose: closeup-thoughtful 0.8,1.02 0.82] [same-as: S006] [zoom-in]
That does not tell us every bargain was fair. It does tell us to take the customers' judgment seriously.

::: S058 | The same kettle and blanket remain in frame while local comparison card prepares to enter. [same-as: S006] [pan-left]
Nor should we assume the price stays the same when we move to a different post.

::: S059 | Four locally typeset place names in two groups, tied to a short source-note paraphrase. [note: Dobbs · p.193, note] [still]
The note above this very list warns us about that. It compares Albany and Moose with York and Churchill.

::: S060 | Wide low river landscape with distant branching water, no false same-day rival shop next door. [same-as: S002] [pan-right]
The difference, in Dobbs's explanation, is competition.

## A price is not a law of nature
::: S061 | Attributed source paraphrase compares nearby competition; no invented numeric York price. [note: DOBBS'S EXPLANATION · 1744] [still]
Dobbs says the standard at York and Churchill is higher, because French traders are not as close to challenge the Company there.

::: S062 | Illustrative canoe on a river bend with secured goods; no French fort invented within view. [same-as: S002] [pan-left]
Around Albany and Moose, he says French competition interferes more with the Company's trade. His price list arrives with an argument attached.

::: S063 | Fort Albany1733scope stamp remains, other undated locations fade; no real price converted. [still]
That helps explain why we have stayed with one place and year. Another post's numbers would make a different shopping basket.

::: S064 | Long low river reach and canoe near shore, enough space conveys travel without unsupported route distance. [same-as: S002] [pan-right]
It does not mean every customer can casually walk down the road to a rival shop. Distance and relationships still matter.

::: S065 | Several adults prepare travel bundles together, no exact population or all-male monopoly asserted. [same-as: S002] [zoom-in]
The post sits inside a much wider world of journeys, households and trading parties. Our counter scene shows only one part.

::: S066 | Distinct 1706 flashback: representative canoe landing. Show the number twenty in a local label; do not generate an exact fleet. [note: ALBANY · 1706 · EARLIER ACCOUNT] [pan-left]
In an earlier account from Albany, seventeen oh-six, a leader named Whatting arrived with twenty canoes.

::: S067 | Coat and wrapped goods offered to an adult leader. No portrait claim, ceremonial costume or generated exact quantities. [note: Preston, citing Williams · 1706 account] [same-as: S066] [zoom-out]
He received a coat, tobacco, knives and a hatchet. This was a gift to a trading-party leader, not our universal shopping allowance.

::: S068 | Coat passes between hands while other party members wait; restrained respectful gesture. [same-as: S066] [pan-right]
The example shows why a list of prices cannot capture every exchange that mattered around the post.

::: S069 | Return to the original 1733 room and fictional customer identities. Do not reuse the 1706 leader as a named 1733 customer. [note: BACK TO FORT ALBANY · 1733] [same-as: S006] [zoom-in]
Leadership and personal relationships belonged in the story alongside bundles and arithmetic. We now return to our seventeen thirty-three example.

::: S070 | Blank ledger beside an equipment parcel, clerk's hand poised to record; explanatory scene without a forged credit contract. [same-as: S006] [pan-left]
There is another complication. Sometimes goods could be advanced before the furs that would repay them arrived.

::: S071 | Customer looks from goods to ledger, composed serious expression. [pose: closeup-thoughtful 0.8,1.02 0.82] [same-as: S006] [zoom-out]
That changes the question from 'what can I take home today?' to 'what will this relationship require later?'

::: S072 | Canoe at landing points toward the river journey; no invented debt prison or coercion scene. [same-as: S002] [pan-right]
Fur as money does not make the future disappear.

## The goods before the furs
::: S073 | Local date interval with 1733 inside the range; do not label a single invention year. [note: Preston · citing Williams1983] [still]
Historian Richard Preston places the beginning of equipment credit at Moose and Albany sometime between seventeen twenty-six and seventeen thirty-seven.

::: S074 | Equipment bundle on counter; adjacent space for a later fur return is empty. No modern loan form. [same-as: S006] [pan-left]
Supplies could be given on the expectation of later fur returns. The arrangement connected more than a single visit.

::: S075 | Simple goods-now and furs-later sequence. No interest rate, deadline or mandatory term. [still]
We should not turn that into a modern payment plan, complete with invented instalments. The evidence describes its own relationships and obligations.

::: S076 | Two distinct customers beside separate bundles; no identical debt labels. [pose: scratch-head 0.8,0.94 0.6] [same-as: S006] [pan-right]
Nor does it mean every person here has the same debt. Our price-list exercise cannot supply somebody's missing account.

::: S077 | Goods and open blank ledger share the composition; hands paused rather than shaking on an agreement. [same-as: S006] [zoom-in]
It does mean a shopper's position could depend on what had already been promised, as well as what was on the counter.

::: S078 | Well-used plain kettle beside a newer one; condition is illustrative, without a documented specific defect. [same-as: S001] [pan-left]
And a household could value the same item differently according to what it already owned or needed.

::: S079 | Six abstract square counters, label illustrative example. No historicalmetalcoins. [still]
Our imagined six-unit budget is deliberately simpler. We are using it to understand the list, not impersonate an actual family's accounts.

::: S080 | Established room with goods separated into two zones; do not show both alternatives as purchased. [same-as: S006] [pan-right]
We have checked quantities, noticed quality and kept the prices in their setting. Now we can make a choice without changing the rules midway.

::: S081 | Single plain wool blanket at left; right clear for six locally drawn unit counters. [same-as: S001] [zoom-in]
The first option is easy to describe: one blanket. The printed price uses all six units.

::: S082 | Knee-length breeches folded neatly; running total added locally, not generated. [same-as: S001] [pan-left]
The other option starts with the breeches at three. That leaves three units to spend on smaller purchases.

::: S083 | Three goods rows combine into a mixed basket, each worth one unit. Render quantities locally. [still]
We can buy the twenty hooks, the eight knives and the two pounds of sugar—one unit for each listed quantity.

::: S084 | Breeches at three plus three one-unit purchases equals six. Blanket at six on the opposite side, separated by OR. [still]
Add them to the breeches, and the total is six. Two very different ways to use the same budget.
