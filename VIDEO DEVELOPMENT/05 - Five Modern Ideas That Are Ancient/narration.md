# 5 “Modern” Ideas That Are Actually Ancient

## A surprisingly familiar day

You have bought lunch, climbed to your apartment and found a warm floor. Then a door opens without anyone touching it. That sounds like an ordinary day. Until the door opens because somebody has lit a fire on an altar. Five familiar ideas, each with an ancient counterpart. Which would you keep? And what is the catch inside every one? Hold that second question. We start with three buildings archaeologists can walk through, then end with two mechanisms preserved in a book. Your ambitious plan is to assemble the most comfortable ancient day you can. Modestly, you call it a countdown. These stops belong to different places and centuries. You cannot walk between them before lunch. Each gets a place, a date, and the same question: what are you getting for your trouble, and who is doing the work? Number five begins with the easiest part of your plan. You are hungry, and somebody else has already prepared food.

## 5. Lunch at the counter

Welcome to Pompeii, in the year seventy-nine. This masonry counter belongs to the kind of business archaeologists call a thermopolium. A thermopolium sold hot food and drink; about eighty are known in Pompeii alone. Convenient, if your greatest culinary talent is feeling hungry. Look at the holes in the counter. They are the mouths of large jars built into the masonry beneath it. The Regio Five counter kept its painted decoration: a sea nymph riding a sea-horse, and animals. A business preserved in actual remains. You can picture yourself arriving, receiving a bowl and leaving the preparation to somebody else. That part feels very familiar. The comparison is prepared food. A modern takeaway's whole business model does not come with it. You can admire the counter without asking whether they have an app. They really do not. And the jars do not supply an exact menu. We should leave their uncertain contents uncertain. Painted animals may relate to what was sold, but that interpretation has limits. For our imagined purchase, the advantage is modest and real enough: you can buy prepared food instead of preparing that meal yourself. It was one option, rather than proof that every resident lacked a kitchen. Your lunch stop passes. A familiar function, supported by a very solid piece of architecture. We cannot quote your bill. The bowl in our imagined purchase will have to do. Somebody has fed you. Now you would quite like a home. So we leave Pompeii for another Roman town, and move forward in time. Your next convenience is waiting above street level. There is, unfortunately, some climbing involved. Number four is an apartment building, and the building has not supplied an elevator.

## 4. A home upstairs

At Ostia, the Insula of the Painted Ceiling belonged to a block combining homes with commercial space below and around them. The archaeological park describes three or four storeys and exterior stairs. Our reconstruction shows three, rather than inventing a certain fourth. People living vertically, sharing a building, with commercial life happening below: you recognize the arrangement. A Roman home does not have to be a detached villa around a private courtyard. Let us step inside the plan. A central, window-lit space opened into other rooms, giving the dwelling a different shape from outside. Archaeologists call the central space a medianum. Look where those tall windows admit daylight. Drawing a row of identical dark boxes would miss something important about this particular interior. Painted decoration survives here too. Upstairs living and commercial neighbours do not automatically mean a building stripped of every decorative ambition. Your imagined apartment has rooms and daylight. You have not needed to acquire an entire villa. The floor plan cannot quote your rent or identify the person handing you a key. And this building changed. Later alterations created smaller, independent apartments, so one drawing cannot honestly represent every stage of its life. Our reconstruction is around the year two hundred. The building's entire history does not happen simultaneously. A home inside a larger urban building: another recognizable solution to fitting people into a town. Then there are those steps. Familiar housing has not abolished familiar effort. Now imagine finishing the climb and discovering a warm floor. For that part of our day, we need another change of address. Number three takes us to Roman Britain, to Aquae Sulis: the place we now call Bath. Follow the heat, not the plumbing.

## 3. The floor is warm

We are visiting the fourth-century bathing complex. Its heated rooms give a very literal version of comfort under your feet. Lift away the floor. A forest of short supports appears underneath. These stacks hold the floor above a cavity. Heat from a furnace travels through that space beneath the occupied room. The Roman Baths preserve examples of these tile supports. They are heating evidence, not broken furniture. The system is called a hypocaust. At Bath, heated air circulated beneath floors and through parts of the walls too. These are passages for hot gases, not modern water pipes zigzagging through concrete. The Roman writer Vitruvius describes building suspended hot-room floors on brick pillars. Someone had worked this problem through carefully. For somebody enjoying the warm room, the useful part stays out of sight. For somebody tending the furnace, it is the whole job. We do not know the attendant's name or circumstances. The fuel and the furnace are the point. The heat has to come from somewhere. Your comfortable floor depends on a service space, a working fire and somebody keeping it going. Another trap at Bath: the Great Bath received naturally hot spring water. Do not turn that spring into a furnace product. Two sources of warmth can share one complex without being the same mechanism. Our countdown is about the heated rooms. Nor does this substantial bathing complex prove that every Roman household enjoyed heated floors. Your floor passes the comfort test. The effort is concentrated somewhere you would rather not stand. So far we have walked through buildings. The final two ideas take us into a different kind of evidence: an ancient engineering book. Number two asks you to put a coin into a machine. It will not serve crisps. The remarkable part is how it releases water.

## 2. The coin is also the switch

Hero of Alexandria describes this device in his Pneumatics. We are showing an interpretation of his written instructions. Hero worked in Roman-era Alexandria. His biography is uncertain, so we invent no installation date. Inside the chest is a water reservoir. A small lid closes the outlet until the mechanism lifts it. In Hero's design, the coin does two jobs: payment, and the weight that moves a lever. One end of the lever carries a plate. The other end holds the lid that closes the outlet. Drop the coin in. Inside, that end of the lever goes down, and the lid at the other end rises. The moving part is small, but it changes the whole machine. The blocked outlet is now open. Water flows through the pipe and out of the chest. No attendant needs to lift that lid for you. When the coin slides off the plate, the balance changes again. The lid drops and closes the outlet. The water stops. Your payment briefly changed the balance, then disappeared into the chest. It is a wonderfully direct arrangement. The coin does not send a message; it physically pushes part of the device. But the description is about water in a religious setting. We have not found an ancient supermarket aisle. A description does not show how widely a device was installed. We cannot give every temple one. The vessel must also contain water. However clever the lever, an empty reservoir cannot provide your next serving. A surviving description is not evidence that every temple installed one. Keep that attached to the surprise. For you, the memorable idea stands: a payment can work a mechanism without anybody taking it by hand.

## 1. Open the door—with fire

Next, a machine responding to heat, with a much more theatrical result. Number one: doors that open when a fire is lit. Before celebrating, look underneath the temple. Hero describes a small temple standing over a hidden mechanism. The fire burns on an altar beside it. There is no person pulling the doors in the instructions. Instead, heat starts a chain of movements below them. We will follow one design, using air, water, a suspended bucket and weights. Hero also describes another arrangement. Our drawing follows the written instructions. It is not an excavated machine. In Hero's design, the altar connects by a tube to a globe partly filled with water beneath it. As the altar grows hot, heated air expands and drives water from the globe through a bent tube. That water enters the suspended bucket. The bucket becomes heavier and descends, tightening chains attached to the door pivots. The chains turn those pivots. Now the doors open, while the person watching above sees none of this hidden work. Heat moves air, air moves water, water changes weight, and weight moves doors. Your apparently self-opening doorway has a very busy basement. It is less effortless once you can see the whole arrangement. Closing needs another part of the design. Opposing chains connect to a counterweight that can pull the doors shut. Hero describes water returning as the system cools. The lighter bucket lets the counterweight close the doors.

## Every convenience has a catch

His explanation of the air contains outdated theory; our diagram follows the intended mechanical sequence. Also notice what this door detects. It responds to the altar's heat, not to a shopper walking towards a sensor. That makes it a striking automatic-door idea, but a rather inconvenient way to enter your kitchen carrying the shopping. Keep the scale honest. The text describes a small temple, not a giant city gate. The excitement is in the actual design: several simple effects arranged so that one event causes something surprising elsewhere. The instructions do not tell us how many installations existed or how reliably they worked. For your imagined day, the doors make a brilliant finale. For a historian, they remain a mechanism described in a text. The design is impressive enough without a false discovery story or an invented portrait of its author. Your ancient comfort list: prepared food, upstairs homes, heated floors, a coin mechanism and heat-operated doors. Now the held question: the catch. In every case somebody out of sight is doing the work: cooking, climbing, feeding the furnace, refilling the chest, tending the altar. None of these proves ancient life was easier for everyone. They show people finding recognizable answers to particular problems. The convenience is yours. The effort belongs to someone else, usually standing somewhere warm, dark and unglamorous. Which one would you keep? You are tempted by the dramatic doors. Your feet are still voting for the warm floor. Back at the little temple, the doors close on their own.
