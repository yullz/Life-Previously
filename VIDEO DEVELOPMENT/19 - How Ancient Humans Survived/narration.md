# Could You Survive One Night With Early Humans?

## One night, no tent, no shop

You are standing on a frozen plain in what will one day be Ukraine. Eighteen thousand years ago. Night. Your survival kit is a hoodie. No shop, no tent, nobody to call, and the nearest tree is a rumour. Nothing here is for sale. Everything you need tonight must be found, made, carried or borrowed. So how did people get through a million years of nights like this, with no metal, no farms, no doctors? And what did every camp have that you lack right now? Hold that one. It is the answer at the end. Six real camps tonight, oldest first, each excavated and dated. Every stop gets a label, because they are very far apart. A fire in a cave, spears by a lake, an injured man, a seam, a bed of grass, a house of bones. Our traveller is invented; the camps are not. Where we only have a reasonable guess, you will hear it said. First problem: you are cold, and your body will not negotiate. Warmth before food, friends or philosophy. The modern reflex is to make a fire. Rub two sticks, wait for the movie moment. The first stop complicates that. Because for most of the human story, nobody knew how to start one. Stop one. South Africa, about one million years ago. Into the dark.

## Stop 1: a fire you cannot make

Wonderwerk Cave. Thirty metres inside, where daylight gives up. On the floor, a small fire that somebody is tending. The evidence is in the ash: burnt bone, stone and sediment in one layer, dated to about a million years ago. Thirty metres from the entrance matters. A wildfire outside cannot reach this far. Someone carried fire here, on purpose, more than once. Here is the awkward part. These people, probably Homo erectus, almost certainly could not make fire. The likely routine: find a grass fire, carry a burning branch home, keep it fed. Lose it, and you wait for the next one. That makes fire a chore shared across days. Somebody feeds it while others sleep. The first thing on this trip that nobody owns alone. Fire bought warmth, light after dark, cooked food, and a reason for a large cat to stay outside. Acheulean stone tools lie in the same layers. The fire-keepers were toolmakers too. You try rubbing two sticks anyway. The locals watch with professional interest, then return to the fire that exists. The lesson is not toughness. It is maintenance: a fragile thing kept alive by many hands, longer than anyone stays awake. The fire keeps you warm tonight. It will not feed you. For that, we jump forward seven hundred thousand years. Stop two. A lakeshore in what is now Germany, about three hundred thousand years ago.

## Stop 2: the stone age was mostly wood

A warm spell between ice ages. A lake, open woodland, alder and willow by the water, wild horses grazing in the open. The mud here kept what almost never survives: one hundred and eighty-seven wooden artefacts. At least ten spears and seven throwing sticks, beside the bones of twenty-odd butchered animals. Mostly horse. You expected a stone age made of stone. Most of what these people held all day was wood. Spruce, pine and larch. None grew at the lake. Someone carried the wood from hills three to five kilometres away. Thirty-five more tools were split from wood, probably for domestic jobs. A workshop by the water, not only a kill site. Taking a wild horse with wooden spears is a group job, and group jobs need trust. The horse is unimpressed by you. The throwing sticks were long-range weapons for smaller prey. Nearly everyone in the group could use one, children included. You are handed a sharpened stick. The joke writes itself, until you see what the sticks have done. Skinning was a major part of the butchery here. Hides left this shore as well as meat. Hides mean warmth later. Who were they? Not our species. Earlier humans, in a Europe our own kind had not reached; we were only emerging in Africa. Hunting big animals with sticks gets people hurt. What happened to the hurt? Stop three: a cave in the Zagros mountains, fifty thousand years ago.

## Stop 3: the man who should not have survived

Shanidar Cave, in what is now northern Iraq. Neanderthals live here: our closest extinct relatives, short, strong and built for cold. Meet Shanidar One. A man of forty to fifty, which here is old. His body tells a long story. His right arm ended above the elbow, probably amputated. His left eye socket was crushed. He limped. He was deaf on one side. A one-armed, half-blind, half-deaf hunter-gatherer in a land of leopards and cliffs. And he grew old. The bones had healed long before he died. Someone fed him, kept him warm and safe, for years. Myth check. 'Survival of the fittest' gets read as 'only the strong survived'. This man is strong evidence against that. We are confident. The researchers' phrase is 'supporting social matrices'. In plain words: he had people. His survival is their work too. Neanderthals used fire, built shelters, made clothing and buried their dead, from about four hundred thousand to forty thousand years ago. You, meanwhile, have a hoodie and a blister. Nobody here finds you impressive, but somebody hands you food. Caring for the injured costs food and time. These groups paid it, and that choice helped humans last. Their line did not continue as a separate people. Ours did. The care was never the weakness. Fire, food and friends sorted, you still need to stop shivering. Stop four: the coast of Spain, forty thousand years ago.

## Stop 4: clothes with seams

Canyars, near today's Barcelona. People of our own species now, working hides in the open. One holds a bone. That bone is a punch board, a backing for pricking holes through hide. Six separate sessions; one made a straight seam. A seam means pieces joined. Joined pieces mean a garment shaped to a body, not a hide thrown over the shoulders. Tailoring, fifteen thousand years before the first eyed needles reach Europe. Your hoodie's zip is looking less clever. Bone needles with eyes appear in East Asia around forty thousand years ago, and in Europe around twenty-six thousand. Clothing is far older. Hide-working tools, and even the genetics of lice, suggest cold-weather clothing by seven hundred thousand years ago. Then Sungir, Russia, about thirty-four thousand years ago: two children buried with tens of thousands of ivory beads and ivory spears. Ten thousand beads are not quick work. They mean time, skill, and people who thought a child was worth it. You are offered a hide tunic with actual seams. It smells like its previous owner. You accept immediately. Warm is not fed. The next camp solves dinner, and it is not what the cartoons promised. Stop five. The shore of the Sea of Galilee, twenty-three thousand years ago, in the coldest stretch of the ice age. Camp rule tonight: dinner is whatever the lake, the reeds and the hills will give. Bring a grinding stone.

## Stop 5: the oldest bed

Six brush huts by the water: tamarisk, willow and oak branches, covered with smaller branches, leaves and grass. Floors dug below ground. Inside the largest hut, four and a half metres by three, someone grinds wild barley and wheat on a flat stone. About one hundred and fifty thousand seeds and fruits were recovered, from over one hundred and forty plant species. You wanted a mammoth steak. You are getting porridge, lake fish, and gazelle if the hunters did well. The bones agree: gazelle over fallow deer, plus fish, birds, hares and tortoises. A wide menu, not one big animal. Thirteen of those plants are weeds, the kind that thrive on trampled ground. The team reads that as small-scale trial cultivation. That is eleven thousand years before farming. Nobody here farms. They are gatherers who noticed what grows where people walk. Now the bed. One floor of this hut was laid with grass in an overlapping pattern, about seven square metres, around a central hearth. A grass mattress, an indoor fire, walls of branches. The excavators read it as a winter stay. You read it as a hotel. The floors also held flint tools, bone tools, beads and wooden objects. People lived here. A hut of branches works where trees grow. Our last stop is a cold steppe where the best building material has four legs. Stop six. Back where you started: the Dnieper basin, eighteen thousand years ago, in daylight this time.

## Stop 6: a house of thirty-seven mammoths

Mezhyrich. Four round structures, twelve to twenty-four metres apart, each about six metres across, each built of mammoth bones. Structure Four alone used bones from at least thirty-seven mammoths, stacked into a wall taller than you. Thirty-seven mammoths. Your first thought is a hunting spree. The evidence is more interesting, and more argued over. The pits hold butchered young mammoths, so some were hunted. Many bones were probably gathered from long-dead animals. How many is disputed. The pits also hold hunting weapons, and ornaments of ivory and bone. Rubbish, tools and treasures, all within a few steps. Inside: a hearth, and at least three floor layers. New dates put the use around eighteen thousand years ago, for up to four centuries. Four centuries is debated, and so is the word 'house'. Dwelling, store, monument, or all three. The latest study says: a dwelling, reused. You look for the door. There is a gap in the bones. The estate agent in your head says 'characterful'. The roof is gone. Whatever covered it did not survive; the top of this picture is a guess, and we say so. Around them, cold forest-steppe, and the age of the mammoth was ending. These were among the last people to build with it. Hunting weapons, hearths, ornaments, bone walls. None of it is one person's work. Back to your night. Night falls on the steppe again, and this time you know where to knock.

## Until now

Same plain, same cold. But now there is a low round shape with smoke rising, and a gap where a door should be. Time for the held question. What did every one of those camps have that you did not? Not muscles. Not a trick. Other people. A fire fed in shifts, a horse hunted together, a wounded man fed for years. Seams sewn, grass laid, bones stacked. Each camp kept alive what no single person could: a flame, a skill, an injured friend, a store of seeds, a house. Survival was a group subscription, paid in food and time. Everyone was both a cost and a carrier of something vital. Your own kit today is the same thing in disguise: shops, heating and medicine are other people's stored-up work. You never meet them. Our species appeared in Africa about three hundred thousand years ago. Everyone alive descends from people who got through nights like this. That includes you. 'Until now' is not a slogan. It is a chain of winters; you are its latest link. So: you, a hoodie, and a frozen plain. Honestly, alone, you would not last the week. With them, you might. That was the whole trick, from the first carried flame to the last mammoth-bone wall. Humans did not survive by being the toughest thing on the steppe. They survived by never facing the problem alone. Now go and knock on the bones. Then choose the next Life, Previously episode on screen. The hoodie stays, for some reason.
