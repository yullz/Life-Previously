# What Did Pirates Do When They Weren’t Raiding Ships?

## Your glamorous new assignment

You have the hat. You have the ship. Unfortunately, somebody has noticed that you also have two perfectly usable hands. Welcome aboard. There is no raid happening today, so you can finally enjoy the peaceful life of a pirate. First, there is work to do. Then some more work. Your dramatic introduction may have raised the wrong expectations. We're using Blackbeard's flagship, Queen Anne's Revenge, as our starting point, off the southeastern coast of North America in seventeen-eighteen. Three questions, then. What did they eat? Who kept the ship working? And did pirates have a bedtime? Keep that last one. Our day aboard is imagined. The tools and accounts are real; nobody left a timetable for this particular morning. A shipwreck can reveal what people used without telling you who had the most annoying shift. It can also spoil a surprisingly cosy picture of pirate life. Not everybody on this ship chose to be here. We'll meet one of those people. First, your enormous appetite has arrived before your first impressive act of seamanship. Being between raids does not mean skipping meals. Someone still needs to supply the pot and prepare its contents. So put the treasure fantasy aside for a moment. Begin with a clue that looks considerably less attractive on a postcard. For our purposes, an extremely useful bone. What can it actually tell us about dinner?

## What reaches the pot

The wreck has produced butchered cattle and pig bones. Meat provisions were part of this ship's story, however unglamorous the leftovers. You might picture Blackbeard eating an enormous roast. That is several enthusiastic steps beyond what these bones prove. They give us neither the recipe nor everybody's portion. Today's pot belongs to our reconstruction. Some provisions could predate the pirates' capture. The ship had a previous life, and its rubbish remembers that. For the moment, keep the simpler conclusion: people aboard needed supplies, preparation and equipment. The food did not arrive already narrated. Our cooking space keeps the pot supported and the fire contained. It is a reconstruction, not a recovered galley plan. Your contribution today is mostly staring hopefully at the bowl. A valuable skill, although perhaps not enough to earn promotion. The evidence keeps pointing towards practical work and the people needed to do it. There was even a named cook caught up in this ship's capture. We will return to how he ended up aboard. Imagine having to prepare those meals while the people waiting for them include men who took your ship. And once the bowl is empty, the ship remains inconveniently made of things that need looking after while you use them. Your next stop is the carpenter. Pirate artifacts include rather more than weapons.

## The floating workplace

The recovered iron gouge has a flattened head made for hammering: a small object with a physical working life. Tools like this worked wood. We can reconstruct their use, but cannot name the hands that held them. You were hoping for a sword. Instead, someone appears to be offering you a practical skill and a piece of timber. The tool collection points to carpentry, sailmaking, rigging and navigation. Keeping a vessel useful required different kinds of knowledge. A raid could bring supplies aboard. It would not turn every passenger into somebody who could repair things. A tool also needs someone who understands it. Your excellent hat has so far failed to supply that knowledge. Some finds remain ambiguous. A large silver needle has competing interpretations; it cannot simply prove sail repairs. On deck, rope and canvas belong to the same practical story. The glamorous ship still depends on unglamorous handling and upkeep. Your hat contributes little here. It does announce a surprisingly theatrical understanding of the job. Now imagine being responsible for one of those repairs. Suddenly, a small detail deserves a great deal of attention. But an expertly repaired ship can still be in the wrong place. Keeping it afloat also involves knowing what is underneath it. This next piece of equipment is not especially elegant. That is unlikely to bother you when the bottom starts getting closer.

## Finding water deep enough

A recovered lead weight, attached to a line, helped find the water's depth. It also helped identify the bottom's character. More useful information than whether the sea looked attractive. Picture our sailor lowering the line. Nothing explodes. No treasure appears. Useful information is arriving in a rather less dramatic form. The weight proves this kind of work, not the depth beneath our imagined scene. Nor does owning the right tool guarantee a safe journey. Equipment is not a magical promise that people never make mistakes. Another small find takes us from the water beside the ship to the problem of working with a chart. Recovered dividers link the wreck to chart navigation: another small tool with a useful job. You lean over the chart. Understanding what to do with it seems harder than simply looking thoughtful beside it. Between the rope and the chart, your adventurous home begins to resemble a demanding workplace. You may be between attacks, but the sea has not agreed to stop being the sea until you feel more enthusiastic. Then there is illness. It rarely arrives at a convenient point in anybody's plans. A medicine chest could matter enough to become the object of a serious demand.

## The chest they actually wanted

In a published account from seventeen-twenty-four, Blackbeard holds ships and prisoners at Charleston and demands a chest of medicines from the authorities. The account describes compliance and the prisoners' release. Medicine belongs here to a story of coercion, not charming shopping. The surviving wreck also contains medical equipment. A brass mortar and pestle belongs to the practical business of preparing substances for treatment. It is tempting to announce that this very tool came from that famous chest. But the connection is uncertain. The tool and the demand survive in different kinds of evidence. Possessing equipment did not mean someone could cure your illness. A treatment kit cannot prove successful treatment. For our imaginary newcomer, that is worrying. Adventure is less enjoyable when you feel terrible. The object story keeps widening: food, repairs, navigation, treatment. These people had bodies and practical needs, however frightening their reputation. Practical needs did not make piracy harmless. Useful people, like useful supplies, could be taken by force. Which brings us back to the cook. He had a different reason for being aboard. His name survives. His story gives this supposed escape from ordinary employment a less comfortable edge. You can take your hat off when the game stops being fun. He did not have that simple choice.

## The cook who did not volunteer

Georges Bardeau was the second cook aboard the French ship La Concorde. When pirates captured her, they forcibly kept him. The vessel became Queen Anne's Revenge. Before that, she had carried enslaved Africans across the Atlantic as part of the slave trade. So this ship's history includes people whose movement was controlled by others. A pirate flag does not turn that into freedom. His skill made him useful. We know he was forcibly retained, not every meal he later prepared. Continued cooking is plausible. It remains an inference, not a surviving diary of his day. That changes our opening question. What pirates did between raids also depended on who you were and how you ended up aboard. Somebody else's adventure could be your captivity. That belongs in the picture alongside the tools, the meals and the famous captain. You can joke about drawing the boring assignment. Actual people aboard did not all share that harmless imaginary situation. There is another easy mistake: imagining that every pirate crew operated under one grand set of rules, like a travelling organisation. The sources contain examples of rules, but an example from another captain cannot quietly become Blackbeard's instruction sheet. For one comparison, leave this ship and consider Bartholomew Roberts's crew, a little later. Their reported rules contain something familiar to anyone told to take the noise outside.

## Did pirates have a bedtime?

Did pirates have a bedtime? One account gives Bartholomew Roberts's crew a rule requiring lights and candles out at eight. Anyone still inclined to drink afterward was supposed to do it on the open deck. Your evening came with conditions. The same account bans gambling at cards or dice for money. A pirate reputation did not automatically mean an absence of rules. A written rule reveals what someone wanted. It does not prove everyone obeyed it. The account even says Roberts's own efforts to curb the drinking failed. Regulation and behaviour are different evidence. These articles belong to Roberts's crew, in a book published in seventeen-twenty-four. They are evidence about that crew's intended rules. The rule tells us where late drinking was supposed to happen. It does not tell us how peacefully every evening ended. Back aboard Queen Anne's Revenge, we still have one clue to examine. This one survived somewhere you might not expect. For conservators, it became a delicate puzzle: scraps of paper that might still connect this wreck with a printed book. No, we have not discovered Blackbeard's personal reading chair. There is one last clue to handle carefully. What would you expect a scrap from a pirate ship to contain? This one points beyond our little working deck. The tiny fragment survived. Now the challenge was identifying what the printed words belonged to.

## A book where you least expect it

Paper survived inside a cannon chamber from Queen Anne's Revenge. Conservators found sixteen tiny fragments and set about identifying them. Some printed words were still readable. Researchers identified them as part of a seventeen-twelve edition of a voyage narrative by Edward Cooke. It was an account of travel around the world. A book about voyages had itself ended up aboard this very eventful ship. We cannot name its reader or establish a cosy book club from those surviving scraps. The discovery is remarkable without that extra story. Printed material was aboard, and its tiny remains survived where paper rarely does. A real object can open a window onto life aboard without supplying every scene beyond it. And this clue brings us back to the question. Between raids, life aboard involved far more than waiting beside a treasure chest. The ship needed food, maintenance, navigation and treatment. Its objects preserve evidence of that work, alongside much harder stories about force. Our workday was invented. Its foundation came from tools, remains and accounts, rather than a costume box. That is what makes the quieter hours worth exploring. They tell you how the famous ship could function at all. You can keep the hat. It has survived the episode admirably.
