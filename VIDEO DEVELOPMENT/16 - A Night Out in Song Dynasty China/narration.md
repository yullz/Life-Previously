# What Was a Night Out Like in China 900 Years Ago?

## The city is still serving

It is dark, you are hungry, and the street ahead is still selling dinner. Welcome to a night out in China, nine centuries ago. There are no electric signs to follow. A pool of lamplight, a cooking stall, and people waiting can make the invitation perfectly clear. We are in Kaifeng around eleven twenty, the capital of the Northern Song. Our guide is a man remembering the city he knew. Meng Yuanlao later wrote about its streets, food, shops and entertainments. His memories give us a remarkable way into an ordinary evening. Our route is invented, but the things drawing us along it come from his account. First food. Then perhaps one more stop. The first problem is choosing. Near Zhou Bridge, Meng lists enough snacks and dishes to make a hungry visitor dangerously indecisive. We can find mutton being cooked and other ready-to-eat food. This is a place to buy something, rather than cook it ourselves. Farther into his descriptions come flatbreads, noodles, different eating houses and regional specialities. Dinner is not one identical bowl for everybody. And the evening has time to develop. Meng says night markets can continue well into the night, with the busiest places continuing until dawn. That does not mean every street is a party. His account also distinguishes quieter places, and people coming home late from their business. For us, the next decision is pleasantly simple. Eat standing near a stall, or go inside and let somebody take our order? Let us try a table. There is a surprise waiting there: the customer may be considerably more demanding than we expected.

## A city with more than one dinner

Inside, the meal need not be whatever one large pot happens to contain. Meng describes restaurants offering different foods and preparations. He mentions Sichuan-style restaurants and establishments serving southern food. People could encounter regional differences without leaving the capital for another province. Those names are not permission to put a modern takeaway menu on the table. We are following the dishes and choices in this source. The important point is the range. A city meal could involve choosing an establishment, then choosing what you wanted it to prepare. At the street stalls, the season changes the offer too. Meng gives separate summer and winter lists near Zhou Bridge. His summer list even includes iced cooling drinks, among them liquorice water. A warm evening could have its own cold temptation. We are not reconstructing the exact recipe, or promising every passer-by could buy one. It is a named offering in his account. That variety is part of the pleasure of reading him. He remembers particular foods, particular places, and the choices between them. It is also a reason to be careful. A long list of things for sale does not tell us what every household could afford. For tonight's imagined visit, we have chosen a modest meal. The server arrives, and our simple order joins everybody else's particular instructions. Somebody wants one preparation. Another wants something different. The challenge is not merely carrying a bowl. It is remembering whose bowl is whose. Meng takes us close enough to hear the process: orders gathered at the tables, then called toward the kitchen for preparation.

## Your order is somebody else's test

The server asks each diner what they want. Hot, cold, or prepared another way: Meng describes customers making their own particular requests. Those choices have to reach the kitchen. The server calls them out, so the people preparing the food know what has been asked for. Then the dishes must return to the right customers. Several tables can become several small tests of memory, balance and attention. Meng admires astonishing feats of bowl carrying. We do not need to stage an impossible balancing act to understand why the skill mattered. Imagine waiting for a hot dish and receiving the wrong order. For the customer, a disappointment. For the server, potentially something worse. The writer says a complaint could bring a reprimand, a loss of wages, or even dismissal. Correct service came with pressure behind it. That is his account, rather than a rule we can impose on every employer. But it changes how we watch the room. Our evening feels leisurely because somebody else is concentrating. A complicated order gives the customer choice and gives the worker more to remember. So when our bowl arrives, let us at least know what we asked for. The person carrying it has enough to keep track of. The scene feels surprisingly recognisable: different tastes, a busy kitchen, and somebody trying to get dinner to the right person while it matters. Still, dinner is only one part of the city after dark. Outside, some establishments are trying to impress you before you even enter. A grand wine house offers a different scale of night out. Our next stop is a look at that spectacle from outside.

## The expensive-looking part of the evening

Meng describes the Fengle wine house with facing buildings, three storeys, connecting bridges and railings. Lamps and candles make the place shine. This is an impression built from his words. We do not possess a floor plan that lets us place every stair and doorway. But the contrast with a modest food stall is real in the account. Some businesses sell a setting as well as something to consume. Imagine arriving here after a quick snack outside. The light, height and activity make a different kind of invitation to spend the evening. We can admire it without pretending our imagined visitor has booked the finest room, or that every city resident could afford the same experience. Meng's city contains choices at different scales. A stall, a table, a grand establishment: they should not all look like the same luxurious hall. Nor should the whole street glow like a festival decoration. Light belongs to lamps, candles and the places where people are using them. Beyond those pools of light, our route grows quieter. We have eaten. We have looked at the grander night out. What about tea? Meng mentions a teahouse with a grotto and a bridge, and people visiting at night to drink tea. Another setting for lingering. The exact arrangement here is imagined. The garden-like features and nighttime visits come from his description, rather than from a modern themed restaurant. For our evening, it offers a pause. Sit a moment, look around, and decide whether this is finally the last stop. Of course, somewhere nearby there is entertainment. Our sensible plan to go home has just acquired one more complication.

## Choosing what to watch

The commercial entertainment districts in Meng's account are places where different activities gather together. Watching a performance need not be an isolated event. Food, other sellers and amusements share the district. You can see how an intended brief visit might become a much longer one. Meng names performers and kinds of performance. Storytelling, puppetry, shadow plays and physical feats all appear in the city's repertoire. This is a list of possibilities, not a recovered programme for our particular night. We are choosing an illustrative stop from that wider world. A storyteller needs no giant painted battlefield for an audience to follow a story. The attraction can be the telling itself. We are not inventing his words or borrowing a later script. The image shows the form, while the source tells us it belonged here. Another option is a shadow performance. Light, a screen and moving figures create something very different from the busy street outside. The mechanics shown are explanatory. We do not claim these are the exact puppets or characters that a named Kaifeng audience watched. For the Visitor, a small stage is enough. For the person giving the performance, our entertainment is skilled work that has to hold attention. And there are other attractions waiting if attention wanders. A district with many things happening can keep offering another reason to stay. We have now eaten, browsed the brighter streets, taken tea and watched a performance. The evening has acquired an impressive number of endings. There is still the walk home. And if we become hungry again, the late-night city may not have finished offering things to buy.

## The people still working

Late customers are not all out for pleasure. Meng explains that people returning late from their affairs create a market for tea sellers. That detail changes the mood of the street. Someone else's night out can overlap with your late journey back from work or business. The cook at the stall is also still cooking. The server at the table is still remembering orders. The performer is still performing. A lively night economy requires more than people enjoying themselves. It requires people preparing, carrying, serving and keeping those activities going. Meng's account also describes fire watch and firefighting equipment. With shops, homes and flames close together, the city had something serious to watch for. Buckets and ladders belong in that description. A modern fire engine does not. Neither does an invented blaze just to make our evening exciting. We do not need a disaster to see the work behind the scene. Even a pleasant evening depends on people paying attention. And we should remember who is telling us about it. Meng is looking back fondly, after warfare forced him away from the city. His preface admits places he never visited, things learned from other people, and gaps that others might help him fill. That makes him a valuable guide, not an all-seeing camera. We can enjoy the details while keeping the limits in view. A city full of desirable things is not proof that everybody lived well. Its pleasures and its labour belong in the same picture. For our imagined visitor, though, one final decision remains. Go home now, or stay long enough to see the next day begin?

## When the evening becomes tomorrow

Meng says some markets close and reopen in the early hours, while the busiest places can keep going through the night. He describes the third and fifth watches. We need not force those night divisions into one exact clock time for every season. So our evening does not end with a switch that turns the whole city off. Different people and businesses have different rhythms. As dawn approaches, Meng describes callers announcing the morning, people rising, markets opening, and food and drink already being sold. The bread shops have their own early sounds of work. The next day's appetite arrives before our imagined night out feels quite finished. That is the final surprise. Stay out long enough, and the people beginning their day can replace the people ending their evening. We came expecting a distant city. Instead, we found familiar decisions: what to eat, where to sit, what to watch, and when to leave. The familiar feeling should not erase the differences. These streets, foods, buildings and performances belong to a particular place and a particular historical account. Nor should it hide the people making the evening possible. Somebody cooked the meal, carried the bowl, and kept the audience listening. Meng remembered a city worth describing in all that detail. Our imaginary night is only one way of following the invitations he left. Would you stop at the food stall, settle into the teahouse, or lose another hour to a performance before finally heading home? Our sensible answer was one quick meal. Kaifeng had other possibilities. By the time we finished looking, somebody was already preparing breakfast.
