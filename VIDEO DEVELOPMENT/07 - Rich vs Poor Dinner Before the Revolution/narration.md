# Rich vs Poor: Dinner Before the French Revolution

## Two tables, one city

Two dinners are being prepared in Paris. At one table, the problem is choosing. At the other, it is making enough. Our rich household can request another dish. The modest household must stretch a small purchase. You arrive in seventeen eighty-three, before the Revolution. Which table would you choose, and what separates them? The obvious answer is more food. But watch the cabbage, the oven and the platter after it leaves the dining room. This will be more interesting than rich people eating meat while everybody else chews a crust. Our households are composites, based on contemporary observations and a cookbook rather than recovered invitations. One guide is the writer Louis-Sébastien Mercier. He had a sharp eye, and equally sharp opinions about the people around him. His sharp opinions need care. Alongside them, the cookbook supplies instructions for actual preparations. First, follow the ingredients. Both tables depend on a city where food is visible, tempting, and unevenly within reach. The Visitor discovers a problem with looking at dinner. Looking is considerably cheaper than buying.

## The market does not belong equally to everyone

Mercier describes abundance at Les Halles: fruit, flowers and food. This city has an appetite. The pastry and roast-food shops are equally persuasive. He lists poultry, hams, pies and sugared cakes displayed where people can see them. The food itself supplies the advertisement. Our Visitor understands the sales pitch immediately. The city can offer plenty of choice. That does not put the same choices inside every customer's basket. Mercier watches buyers for wealthy households taking what they want, while smaller households compete for what their money can cover. He blames luxury fiercely. That is social criticism, not a complete explanation of every food price. A more useful detail is his attention to small purchases. Having less money can mean buying a fragment rather than a convenient larger quantity. His poor household's little sausage is a limited treat, rather than an exercise in choosing an impressive dinner. For our composite meal, we pair the small purchase with bread. The portion is illustrative, not a recovered family account. At the other end of the comparison, a buyer can think about the next dish, the sauce and how the table will look. Same market, different choices. One basket carries possibilities; the other needs to make a small purchase serve. Now take the smaller basket home. Before calling its contents boring, notice how much depends on what else the household can manage.

## A small dinner is not the same as no food culture

Bread belongs at the centre of this table. Mercier calls it a principal food of the poor in large French towns. Beside our loaf sits the small sausage portion. We will not make the diners into a joke. They are choosing within limits. Our Visitor has come to understand, rather than write a restaurant review. The amount of meat is modest here. But the claim that ordinary Parisians never ate meat fails against Mercier's own descriptions. He names soup and boiled beef at dinner, with other beef preparations in the evening. He also mentions Sunday mutton. That describes a meal pattern, not everyone's ability to afford it. Poverty has degrees. A household scraping together a small purchase is not interchangeable with a comfortable family employing a servant. Our single table is a comparison, not an unchanging national menu. The room for adjustment is narrow. Another dish requires another ingredient from somewhere. And having food in the room is not the final step. Cooking needs a place, heat, utensils and somebody doing the work. Keep that hearth in mind. Later, another Parisian household will solve a cooking problem by carrying its dinner out of the house. The bread is cut and the small dish shared. Modest food can matter greatly to its diners. The rich table looks more generous. Follow it behind the scenes, where somebody prepares the food. Follow the Visitor through that door. A surprisingly ordinary vegetable is about to acquire a very elaborate supporting cast.

## What wealth adds to the plate

Our affluent dinner includes roast poultry. Both Mercier's shops and Menon's earlier cookbook put poultry comfortably within this culinary world. The book appeared in seventeen fifty-five. We choose one period recipe for our imagined seventeen-eighty-three household. Its roast section discusses preparing poultry and serving it with the right colour. The finished platter is the endpoint of skilled handling. At our imagined table, there are several dishes to choose among. The diners have room for preferences beyond making one purchase last. Mercier mocks affluent diners demanding novelty even when surrounded by elaborate food. His spoiled diner is a satirical figure. Our composite table does not turn that caricature into every wealthy person. A simpler contrast is visible right here: a person serving, dishes arriving, and diners who can remain at the table. In this composite household, somebody else's work keeps dinner moving. That work does not appear as a separate ingredient on the plate. The cabbage is an unlikely luxury. A plain vegetable announces less than a decorated room. Now open Menon's recipe. It turns that cabbage into a garnish around meat, with several stages before it reaches the table. Its sauce adds ingredients and operations. The vegetable's name gives you only part of the dinner. So the wealthy version of an ordinary ingredient may carry a kitchen's time and resources inside something that looks deceptively simple. You cannot read all that work just by looking at the dish. We need to rewind from the table to the pan. This reveals more than simply comparing which household has the larger piece of meat.

## Follow the cabbage

Menon's seventeen-fifty-five recipe begins by blanching the cut cabbage. This is our period example, not a recovered family's menu. The cabbage is cooled and squeezed before further cooking. Several actions hide behind one word on a menu. Next comes braising. The recipe gives the vegetable its own preparation before placing it around the meat. Even the arrangement has a purpose: the cabbage becomes a garnish around another dish, rather than the whole meal. But we have not finished. A separate sauce brings in ham, mushrooms, and garlic studded with a clove. These ingredients do work in the recipe. They are not decorations beside the pan. The recipe then adds broth and coulis. The pan is carrying more than the cabbage's own flavour. It is reduced to a sauce and strained. A plain-looking vegetable has brought another pan and a sieve into the story. Pepper finishes this part of the preparation. We have crossed several stages without needing a fantastically rare centrepiece. Extra ingredients, equipment and heat give this cook more options than the other table's small purchase. The finished plate conceals much of that process. You see cabbage; the kitchen has handled a chain of preparations. Our Visitor distrusts the word 'simple.' It can describe a result while ignoring the effort. We have demonstrated one cookbook's recipe, without ordering it for every wealthy Parisian. Now change the problem. Suppose you have bought meat, but your home offers little more than a hearth.

## Dinner goes out to be cooked

Mercier describes small households sending meat to pastry-shop ovens. Buying the food did not mean owning the best place to cook it. This household can afford the meat. It need not be as poor as our first composite. Their solution is practical: carry the dish to somebody whose oven is already part of a working business. The household pays for cooking and saves on wood at home. Fuel belongs in the cost of dinner too. This arrangement links the home kitchen to a commercial one. Our imagined dinner has left the house without becoming a restaurant outing. Mercier grumbles about quality and handling. Paying someone else did not guarantee a perfect roast. But the service itself is revealing. Space and fuel can matter alongside the ingredients in deciding how a household eats. If you only compare two finished plates, that journey to the oven disappears. Follow the dish, and another part of the city appears. Our wealthy household brings work behind its dining-room door. Another household carries food to where the heat is. These are different ways of arranging labour and equipment. A meal has a history before anybody sits down to eat it. And its history may continue afterwards. At the richer table, we have been following the diners. Now watch the person clearing away. The food leaving the room is not necessarily finished with. There is a third place at this comparison that we have barely considered.

## The third place at the table

Mercier makes a pointed observation: servants in wealthy households could eat better than some people living in modest bourgeois homes. His comparison is about access to food, not a claim that serving somebody gave you control over their money or household. The contrast complicates our two tables. The person carrying a dish may have a relationship to its contents beyond simply transporting it. This is Mercier's observation, not a generous leftover allowance guaranteed to every servant. There is another route for food after dinner. Menon's cookbook explicitly begins one recipe with leftover roast poultry. That poultry becomes a filling, enclosed between thin pieces of pastry. The finished rissoles are shaped as little crescents. They are fried to a golden colour. Poultry from an earlier meal can return to the table in another form. The recipe demonstrates reuse. It cannot tell us what happened to every plate in Paris. So our rich-and-poor comparison has acquired cooks, shop ovens, servants and a second use for food that looked finished. We have moved beyond a feast facing an empty plate. Inequality remains, expressed through more than two dinners at first glance. Our Visitor admires the cooking while noticing who supplies the time and who must stretch a purchase.

## What the two dinners reveal

Back at the modest table, the meal is shared. Back at the richer one, people have had more room to choose. The difference includes how much arrives, but also who prepares it, where it cooks, and what happens to the remains. A small piece of sausage disproved one easy stereotype. A cabbage showed how much effort can hide inside an ordinary ingredient. The shared oven added fuel and space. The service table showed why a person's position alone does not describe every mouthful. These dinners do not explain the entire Revolution. They help us see unequal lives in the same city before it. Which part would you have missed: the hidden sauce, the trip to the oven, or the next person to eat? Our Visitor has one request. Before calling a dish simple, let him see how many pans somebody has washed for it. This is Life, Previously. Dinner's story begins before it arrives, and continues after the guests stop watching.
